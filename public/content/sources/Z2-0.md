> House-curated overview of unparticle physics and scale-invariant sectors.
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the cited authors.

Howard Georgi proposed "unparticle physics" in 2007: the possibility that the Standard Model coexists with a hidden sector governed by **scale invariance** (conformal symmetry), whose excitations don't look like ordinary particles at all.

Ordinary particles have fixed masses. Scale them, and you can tell — a fixed rest mass breaks scale symmetry. In a scale-invariant (conformal) sector there is no mass scale at all: the physics is identical under any rescaling, like a fractal. The excitations of such a sector cannot be counted as discrete particles with definite masses; they have "fractional" effective dimensions, and the energy spectrum behaves like a non-integer number of massless species. Georgi's provocative suggestion was that this hidden sector could couple weakly to ordinary matter (through "unparticle" operators) and produce signatures unlike anything in the standard zoo: events with missing energy and momentum distributions corresponding to a continuous, non-integer effective particle number — searchable at colliders like the LHC.

The concept drew on older scale-invariant ideas: currents in conformal field theory, the physics of critical points (at phase transitions, correlation lengths diverge and the system loses its characteristic scale — which is why critical phenomena exhibit power laws and universality), and Georgi's own unification work. Experimental signatures looked for include unusual missing-energy distributions and spectral shapes in B-meson decays, Z-boson mixing, and — in cosmological applications — scale-invariant "unparticle stuff" behaving like dark matter or dark energy with unusual couplings to gravity. No evidence for unparticles has been found; they remain a theoretical possibility.

Why the idea is genuinely interesting beyond exotic physics: scale invariance is the mathematical cousin of power-law behavior. The same formal property — no preferred scale — appears in fractals, phase transitions, turbulence, Zipf's law, scale-free networks, and the "fat tail" distributions of markets and cities. A scale-free sector cannot be described by counting typical objects, because there is no typical scale; distributions stretch across all magnitudes. This connects the unparticle concept to a much broader pattern: systems at criticality or under multiplicative dynamics lose characteristic scales and demand statistics built around power laws and renormalization-group thinking, not averages and variances.

Renormalization group (Wilson, 1970s) is the formal machinery: flows in scale-space, fixed points, and universality classes that explain why wildly different systems (magnets, fluids, early universe) show identical critical behavior because the fine details wash out under rescaling. Cosmologically, scale invariance is central too — the primordial density fluctuations that seeded galaxies are observed to be nearly scale-invariant (Harrison–Zel'dovich spectrum), a key piece of evidence for inflation; the universe's structure began as a fractal-like, no-scale distribution. Recent speculative work periodically invokes scale-invariant sectors or unparticle-like couplings to explain dark energy or anomalous cosmic expansion, though such proposals remain testable-and-unverified. The recurring lesson: when a system stops having a characteristic size, duration, or mass, normal counting breaks — and you're in power-law territory where the rare extremes dominate.
