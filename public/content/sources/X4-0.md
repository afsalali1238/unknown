> House-curated overview of algorithmic governance and opacity.
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the cited authors.

Algorithms increasingly make or mediate high-stakes decisions — sentencing and bail recommendations, hiring screening, loan approvals, content moderation, credit and insurance pricing, medical triage — producing two distinct problems: **opacity** (the real reasons for a decision cannot be inspected, even by the operator) and **determinism at scale** (one model's blind spots become one rule applied uniformly across millions of cases).

Opacity has three sources (Burrell's typology). First, trade secrecy: the firm declines to disclose. Second, technical illiteracy: law and policymakers cannot read the system. Third — and genuine — **opacity of learned models**: deep neural networks encode millions of parameters optimized by gradient descent; no human can state the rule producing an individual output, and even knowing the full weights does not yield an explanation humans can follow. Post-hoc explanation tools (saliency maps, LIME) are approximations that can be gamed and are not the causal reasons.

The structural harms follow from well-documented mechanisms:

- **Bias by training data.** Models replicate and launder historical patterns: resume screeners inherit past hiring discrimination (the famous Amazon case), risk scores (COMPAS) were shown by ProPublica to misclassify Black defendants as higher risk at greater rates while balancing overall accuracy — a mathematical fact (fairness metrics are mutually incompatible) hidden under "the data says so."
- **Accountability laundering.** When the algorithm decides, no official is responsible: "the system" rejected you, the model recommended the sentence. Human discretion re-enters as rubber-stamping (automation bias — human raters follow machine suggestions even when wrong).
- **Opacity as a barrier to due process.** Law traditionally requires stating reasons a person can contest. An unexplainable score cannot be appealed with evidence; the subject doesn't know what they're accused of.
- **Scale and uniformity.** A biased human harms case by case; a biased model harms the whole population of cases, identically, instantly — and the uniformity feels like objectivity.
- **Optimization pressure.** A model optimized on a proxy (arrests, clicks, loan default) reshapes the world toward the proxy (Campbell's Law / Goodhart), creating feedback loops: predictive policing puts police where arrest data says crime is, which generates arrest data.

The classic dystopian and literary readings (from *The Trial*'s unexplainable bureaucracy to panopticon theory) converge with modern governance proposals: rights to explanation and human review (EU GDPR's limited version, EU AI Act's risk-tiering), audits, disclosure of training distributions, bans on certain uses (social scoring, emotion recognition), and "algorithmic hygiene" of public administration. The deeper argument, following thinkers like Frank Pasquale (*The Black Box Society*), is that opacity in power is the real threat, not automation as such — algorithms in public hands with contestable decisions are governance infrastructure; black boxes are unappealable government. For citizens the practical stances: demand reasons and data, assume "neutral technology" frames political choices, and remember that a rule you cannot inspect or appeal is definitionally arbitrary in its effects — however scientific its surface.
