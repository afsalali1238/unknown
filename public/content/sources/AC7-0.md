The Monty Hall problem is a probability puzzle, named after the host of the television game show *Let's Make a Deal*. It was originally posed in a 1975 letter by statistician Steve Selvin to *The American Statistician*, and became famous when reader Craig Whitaker's version appeared in Marilyn vos Savant's "Ask Marilyn" column in *Parade* magazine in 1990:

> Suppose you're on a game show, and you're given the choice of three doors: Behind one door is a car; behind the others, goats. You pick a door, say No. 1, and the host, who knows what's behind the doors, opens another door, say No. 3, which has a goat. He then says to you, "Do you want to pick door No. 2?" Is it to your advantage to switch your choice?

Vos Savant answered that the contestant should switch. Under the standard assumptions — the host always opens a door the contestant didn't pick, always reveals a goat (never the car), and always offers the switch — switching wins the car two-thirds of the time, while staying with the original choice wins only one-third of the time.

The reasoning: when the player first chooses, there is a two-thirds chance the car is behind one of the two unchosen doors. The host, by deliberately opening a goat door among those two, doesn't change that probability — he collapses it onto the single remaining door. Switching is therefore not the same as picking randomly between two doors; the host's action conveys information the player didn't have at the start. Vos Savant offered an intuition pump: imagine a million doors. You pick door No. 1; the host, who knows where the car is, opens ninety-eight goat doors and leaves only door No. 777,777 closed. You'd switch fast.

The counterintuitive answer provoked an extraordinary reaction: roughly 10,000 readers — including nearly 1,000 with PhDs — wrote to *Parade* insisting vos Savant was wrong, even after explanations, simulations, and formal proofs. Paul Erdős, one of the most prolific mathematicians in history, remained unconvinced until shown a computer simulation. The episode became a famous case study in how strongly human intuition resists base-rate and conditional-probability reasoning. The puzzle is mathematically related to the earlier three prisoners problem and Bertrand's box paradox.
