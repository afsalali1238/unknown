The efficient-market hypothesis (EMH) is a theory in financial economics stating that asset prices reflect all available information. A direct implication is that it is impossible to "beat the market" consistently on a risk-adjusted basis, because prices should only react to genuinely new information — which, by definition, cannot be anticipated.

The idea that financial returns are difficult to predict predates EMH — Louis Bachelier argued for it in 1900, and Paul Samuelson formalized related results — but it is associated above all with Eugene Fama, whose influential 1970 survey, "Efficient Capital Markets: A Review of Theory and Empirical Work" (*Journal of Finance*), organized the theory and the evidence. The mechanism is straightforward: if information about a stock's value is widely available and the price does not yet reflect it, investors trade on it, moving the price until the information no longer yields an edge. Competition among informed traders does the work of setting prices.

Fama distinguished three forms. **Weak-form efficiency**: prices reflect all information contained in past prices and volume — so technical analysis (chart patterns, momentum rules) cannot earn excess returns. **Semi-strong form**: prices reflect all publicly available information — financial statements, earnings announcements, macroeconomic news — so fundamental analysis cannot consistently beat the market once the news is out; prices adjust within minutes. **Strong form**: prices reflect even insider information. Empirical evidence broadly supports weak and semi-strong efficiency for large liquid markets; strong form does not hold, which is why insider trading is profitable — and illegal.

Crucially, efficiency does not say prices are always correct or that returns are unpredictable. It says you cannot know in advance *which way* prices will be wrong: mispricing is as likely to be against you as for you. Prices can drift predictably over long horizons because of time-varying risk premia (crisis risk earns a higher expected return), and bubbles and crashes exist ex post — but identifying them ex ante is exactly what the hypothesis says is hard. Alfred Cowles showed in the 1930s that professional investors as a group failed to outperform; later work on mutual funds found most active managers underperform index funds after fees, with about the success rate you'd expect from chance.

Since the 1990s research has focused on "anomalies" — deviations from specific risk models: momentum, value, the size effect. Fama himself argues many anomalies are compensation for risk or data mining; behaviorists (Robert Shiller, Richard Thaler) argue they reflect systematic cognitive error. The EMH remains the baseline: not because markets are perfectly smart, but because consistent outperformance requires being smarter than the consensus of smart, motivated, well-capitalized competitors — and the evidence says almost nobody is.
