Dunbar's number is a suggested cognitive limit on the number of people with whom an individual can maintain stable social relationships — relationships in which each person knows who the others are and how they relate. It was proposed in the 1990s by British anthropologist Robin Dunbar, who found a correlation across primate species between neocortex size and average social group size, then extrapolated from human brain size to a predicted stable group of about 150 people (with a confidence interval of roughly 100 to 230).

The reasoning begins with why primates have large brains for their bodies. Primates are intensely social, and social cohesion is maintained by social grooming — the close, time-consuming work of picking through one another's fur, which bonds partners into protective cliques. The number of partners an individual can groom in this way appears to be limited by the processing capacity of the neocortex: more relationships mean tracking who is friends and rivals with whom, remembering favors and slights, and managing one's own position in the network. Across 38 primate genera, mean group size tracks neocortical volume.

Plug human brains into the same regression and it predicts a "mean group size" of about 148, rounded to 150. Dunbar then checked the prediction against human societies: Neolithic farming villages, the splitting point of Hutterite religious communities, the standard size of Roman military centuries and modern military companies, and the upper bound of hunter-gatherer bands all cluster near 150. Groups larger than this, the argument goes, cannot be held together by personal knowledge of one another and require formal rules, laws, hierarchy, and enforced norms to stay stable. Dunbar informally defined the group as "the number of people you would not feel embarrassed about joining uninvited for a drink if you happened to bump into them in a bar."

Holding a group of 150 together is expensive in bonding time: Dunbar estimated as much as 42% of the group's waking time would be needed for grooming-equivalent social effort — which is why such sizes appear mainly under survival pressure (subsistence villages, nomadic tribes, military units). His further proposal in *Grooming, Gossip, and the Evolution of Language* is that language evolved as a cheap form of grooming: by talking about who did what to whom — gossip — humans can service many relationships simultaneously and at a distance, maintaining cohesion without spending half the day in physical contact.

The number has become a famous rule of thumb for companies, communities, and social networks — Gore-Tex famously caps its plants at 150 employees; online studies find the core friend network averages close to 150 despite contacts lists in the thousands. Critics note the regression is coarse, modern institutions extend trust through systems, and weak-tie networks scale far beyond it. But the durable finding is that *strong* relationships have a hard ceiling set by the size of the brain that maintains them — beyond it, community becomes crowd.
