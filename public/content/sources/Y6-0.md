> House-curated overview drawing on the problem of moral luck and Stoic philosophy.
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the original authors.

The "asymmetry of cosmic justice" is the observation that the world does not distribute outcomes according to desert — and that humans nonetheless cannot help expecting it to. The good suffer, the wicked prosper, effort goes unrewarded and luck is mistaken for virtue, on every observable scale from individual lives to the fates of nations. The Book of Job is the oldest sustained treatment: an upright man is ruined without explanation, and his friends' insistence that suffering must imply sin is exposed as false piety. "The rain falls on the just and the unjust alike" is the same observation from the opposite side.

Philosophy has named several pieces of the asymmetry. The **problem of moral luck** (Bernard Williams, Thomas Nagel): we praise and blame people partly for outcomes they did not control — the reckless driver who kills a child is judged a monster; the identical driver whose road was empty gets a ticket. Circumstantial luck (who happened to be born where, when, into what), constitutive luck (temperament, innate dispositions), and resultant luck together mean most of what we call virtue and reward is downstream of accidents. Adam Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments* noted this asymmetry between sympathy for fortunate and unfortunate people as a corruption of moral judgment we are built for.

The Stoic response — and the practical core of the idea — is to redefine the terms of justice. Control is dichotomous: some things are up to us (judgments, choices, efforts, integrity), most are not (body, reputation, wealth, other people's actions, the outcomes of our plans). The universe's ledger tracks cause and effect, not merit; expecting it to pay for virtue is the category error that generates resentment, envy, and despair. Marcus Aurelius: "Do not act as if you had ten thousand years to throw away." Justice as cosmic payout is unavailable; justice as *your own conduct* is the only ledger fully in your hands. The asymmetry cuts both ways and that is the solace: when you are unfairly dealt with, the world has not broken a promise, because it made none; when you are rewarded without deserving it, gratitude and humility are the honest response rather than self-congratulation.

A modern economic version: markets and histories are selective pressures, not judges. They reward fit with current conditions — timeliness, product-market fit, being born into the right technical wave — with compounding payoffs that look like virtue after the fact (survivorship bias, narrative fallacy). This does not make effort pointless; it makes the *internal goods* of effort (skill, integrity, dignity) the secure ones and the external goods (fame, wealth) treated as windfall to be used well.

The operational conclusions: grade yourself on inputs you control (effort, honesty, kindness, craft) rather than outcomes you don't; judge others leniently because their outcomes, good or bad, contain large luck components — envy of winners is mostly envy of dice; don't defer to success as proof of wisdom; and build systems (legal accountability, insurance, redistribution, safety nets) precisely because natural outcomes are insensitive to desert — human justice is the remedy for cosmic injustice, not a description of how the world already works.
