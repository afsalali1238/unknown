A Vickrey auction — also called a sealed-bid second-price auction — is an auction in which bidders submit sealed bids without seeing one another's, the highest bidder wins, but the price paid is not their own bid: it is the *second*-highest bid. It was described academically by Columbia professor William Vickrey in his 1961 paper "Counterspeculation, Auctions, and Competitive Sealed Tenders" (*Journal of Finance*), for which Vickrey won the 1996 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences — though the mechanism had long been used by stamp collectors since 1893, and Johann Wolfgang von Goethe ran a second-price sealed-bid sale of a manuscript in 1797.

The remarkable property is **incentive compatibility**: in a Vickrey auction with private values, each bidder maximizes their expected outcome by bidding exactly their true valuation of the item. Truth-telling is a *dominant strategy* — you cannot do better by shading your bid up or down, regardless of what others do.

The proof is simple. Your bid only determines whether you win; it never determines what you pay (that is set by the second-highest bid). Bidding above your true value can only make you win in cases where the second-highest bid exceeds what the item is worth to you — winning at a price above your valuation, a loss you could have avoided. Bidding below your true value can only make you lose in cases where the second-highest bid was below your valuation — forfeiting a purchase you would have made at a profit. Since your bid sets the ceiling on your participation but not the price, honesty is optimal.

This contrasts with a first-price sealed-bid auction, where you pay what you bid, so every bidder shades downward according to guesses about others' valuations — strategic gaming in which less sophisticated bidders are penalized and revealing your true value is a mistake. The Vickrey design makes the clever strategy and the honest strategy the same, which is why mechanism designers prize it: it is a piece of market architecture that elicits truthful information.

Generalized variants are enormously influential. The **generalized second-price auction** ran Google's and Yahoo's search-ad markets for years (though it is not fully incentive compatible), and the **Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanism** extends the principle to multiple goods, charging each winner the opportunity cost their win imposes on others. The weakness of the pure Vickrey auction is that it does not allow price discovery when bidders are unsure of their own valuations, and it performs poorly under collusion; it is also rare in literal form in consumer markets. But as the foundational example of an incentive-compatible mechanism, it taught economics that rules can be designed so that rational self-interest reveals rather than conceals the truth — an insight that underpins auction design, platform markets, and algorithmic resource allocation.
