> Peter Kaufman's talk on multidisciplinary thinking, archived via Farnam Street.
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the author.

Peter Kaufman (Glenair, friend/editor of Munger materials) presents the Munger-style case for a multidisciplinary latticework of mental models. The core metaphor: the world is a multidimensional object; each discipline looks at it from one side. Looking from only one side — one department, one professional lens — makes most of reality invisible to you.

The argument: the big problems don't come in departments labeled "economics" or "psychology" or "biology." Markets involve incentives (economics), bias and crowding (psychology), competition and selection (biology), scaling and feedback (systems/engineering), and limits (physics). A thinker with one model explains everything in that model's terms; a thinker with a latticework of 80–90 big, interlocking models — compounding, incentives, availability, scaling laws, selection, redundancy, margins of safety, probability — sees which tools apply where.

Kaufman's memorable triad of learning domains: physics and engineering (the rules that can't be violated, and the margin-of-safety principle of over-engineering), biology (evolution, adaptation, niches, selection — the universe we actually live in), and human history/psychology (the recurring patterns of crowds, incentives, fear and greed). Read across all three, he argues, and the same lessons show up in different vocabularies — convergence that signals a genuine principle.

The method: take the foundational courses/books of each field (not academic frontiers, but the well-proven core), distill each big idea to one or two sentences, and collect them into a working set you actually use — crossing disciplines whenever you analyze a problem. The point is not erudition but avoiding the "man with a hammer" failure of single-lens expertise in a world that rewards seeing the whole object.
