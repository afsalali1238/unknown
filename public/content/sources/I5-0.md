> House-curated overview of Battery Ventures' cloud/open-source infrastructure research series.
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the cited authors.

Battery Ventures' recurring cloud infrastructure research — the *OpenCloud* series and its *State of Cloud* and *State of Open Source Software* reports — maps how the software infrastructure market evolves, and especially how open source eats into enterprise IT budgets.

The recurring findings:

- **Open source as market entry and standardization.** Projects (Linux, Kubernetes, Docker, Kafka, Elasticsearch, Terraform, PostgreSQL) start in developer communities, often solving problems inside hyperscalers (Google publishing Kubernetes), then become defaults; commercial firms monetize via managed hosted versions, enterprise features, support, and licensing (Redis Labs, HashiCorp, Confluent, GitLab patterns).
- **The cloud pricing arbitrage lifecycle.** New software categories begin as proprietary hosted products (cloud high-margin); OSS versions appear and commoditize the category over a few years; cloud providers then either offer it as a managed service or acquire companies. Battery's maps follow "open-source companies worth billions" growing each cycle.
- **Cloud unit economics and cloud repatriation.** Costs on public clouds scale superlinearly for certain workloads; once usage is large and stable, running own infrastructure or moving to specialized colocation/RISC-based hardware becomes cheaper — Dropbox's "Magic Pocket," 37signals's exit from cloud in 2023, large-model training being built by owners of data centers (OpenAI–Oracle–nVidia partnerships, xAI's Memphis supercomputer).
- **The AI infrastructure wave, 2023 onward.** Compute clusters, GPU allocation, model serving, and vector/data infrastructure became the new platform layers; open source models (Llama, Mistral, DeepSeek) applied the OSS commoditization pattern to the model layer itself, while hardware and energy became binding constraints — Battery's recent reports map AI infrastructure spending and the "AI-native" software categories emerging on top.

Strategic implications the reports draw for operators and investors: open source functions as distribution and trust mechanism — community adoption precedes commercial conversion; the durable business models are (1) managed hosting (where cloud providers compete too), (2) enterprise-layer features on open core, and (3) switching costs via workflow/data gravity; margins survive where open source cannot easily replicate operational investment (compliance, scale, SLAs, integration) — but licenses get tested repeatedly when platforms over-monetize.

For infrastructure builders the recurring advice from the series: own the community, convert a small percentage, and expect hyperscalers as both competitors (they'll host your open-source code) and partners (marketplaces, commit programs); price for the long term against managed-service substitution; treat the cloud as a strategic stage — early on rent infrastructure for speed, reconsider owning at scale; and watch the OSS layer of each new platform transition (mobile OSS of the 2010s, AI OSS now) because that layer indicates where margins will be commoditized first.
