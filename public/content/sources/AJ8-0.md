*Theory of Games and Economic Behavior* was published by Princeton University Press in 1944, written by the mathematician John von Neumann and the economist Oskar Morgenstern. It is the foundational text that created game theory as a field. Economist Richard Stone called it the most important publication in economics since Keynes's *General Theory*; Arthur Copeland saw it as making economics an exact science.

The book grew from von Neumann's 1928 paper "Zur Theorie der Gesellschaftsspiele" ("On the Theory of Parlor Games"), which proved the minimax theorem: for any two-player zero-sum game — situations in which one player's gain is exactly the other's loss — there exists a pair of strategies (possibly randomized, or "mixed") such that each player guarantees themselves the best result they could possibly achieve against a rational opponent. Von Neumann and Morgenstern extended the analysis from parlor games to economic life itself, arguing that any situation where one person's optimal choice depends on others' choices is a game and can be studied mathematically.

The book moves from the formulation of the economic problem as strategic interaction, to zero-sum two-person games (where minimax reigns), to three-person and n-person games, where players can form **coalitions**. This was a decisive move: in a game with three or more players, two can agree to coordinate against the third, so the theory must study which alliances form, how the spoils are divided among the members, and whether coalitions are stable. The authors modeled this with the "characteristic function," which assigns to every possible coalition the maximum value its members can guarantee together — reframing bargaining, cartels, and even political power as questions about stable coalition structures.

The 1947 second edition added the famous appendix giving an **axiomatic treatment of utility**: starting from a small set of consistency conditions on rational preferences over gambles, von Neumann and Morgenstern proved that a rational agent behaves as if maximizing the expected value of a numerical utility function. This established expected utility as the formal model of rational choice, and the basis for decision theory, behavioral economics' later departures from it, and much of microeconomics. Jimmie Savage extended the framework to subjective probability in 1954, linking rational choice with Bayesian inference.

The limitations of the 1944 framework are part of its legacy. It solves zero-sum games cleanly but only begins the analysis of non-zero-sum interactions — where both players can gain or both lose, and where cooperation problems like the prisoner's dilemma (formalized by Tucker around 1950) live. John Nash's equilibrium concept (1950) generalized the minimax solution to many-player, non-zero-sum games. But everything that followed — Schelling on commitment and conflict, Axelrod on iterated cooperation, auction theory, mechanism design, the application of game theory to biology, politics, and AI — stands on von Neumann and Morgenstern's recognition that strategy, not just supply and demand, is the heart of economic life, and that it can be a mathematics.
