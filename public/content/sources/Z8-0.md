Fusion ignition is the point at which a nuclear fusion reaction becomes self-sustaining: the energy given off by the reaction heats the fuel more rapidly than it cools, so that the fusion's own self-heating removes the need for external heating. It is quantified by the Lawson criterion and by the fusion energy gain factor, Q. Ignition is distinct from *breakeven*, which compares energy output to the energy used to heat the fuel but ignores energy lost to the surroundings; ignition is the threshold a practical energy-producing design must cross.

On December 5, 2022, the National Ignition Facility (NIF) at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in California achieved ignition by the energy-gain definition for the first time in a laboratory: 192 laser beams delivered about 2.05 megajoules of ultraviolet energy to a tiny capsule of deuterium-tritium fuel, which produced about 3.15 megajoules of fusion energy — a gain of roughly 1.5 over the laser energy delivered. (The fusion-heating threshold defined by the Lawson criterion had been crossed at NIF the previous August.) The result was announced by the U.S. Department of Energy on December 13, 2022, and hailed as one of the landmark scientific achievements of the century: for the first time, a controlled fusion experiment produced more energy from fusion than the laser energy that drove it.

NIF uses inertial confinement fusion. The laser beams rapidly heat the inner surface of a gold cylinder (a hohlraum) containing a peppercorn-sized fuel capsule; the hohlraum emits X-rays that blow off the capsule's outer surface; the rocket-like blowoff compresses the remaining fuel inward to about twenty times the density of lead and temperatures of roughly 100 million degrees Celsius; thermonuclear burn then spreads rapidly through the compressed fuel. It is the same physics that powers a hydrogen bomb — where a fission "sparkplug" compresses fusion fuel — but in the laboratory a laser rather than a weapon provides the compression, and the fuel amount is minuscule.

The result is a scientific milestone rather than a power-plant blueprint. The 2.05 MJ of laser light required hundreds of megajoules of wall-plug electricity to produce, the lasers fire roughly once a day rather than the ten times per second a power plant would need, and the target capsules are exquisitely expensive. But ignition proved experimentally that a controlled fusion target can release more energy than is put into it — the threshold the field had pursued for seventy years — and it opened the path to both stockpile-stewardship science and the long-horizon engineering problem of fusion electricity. Stars ignite at around fifteen million kelvin because they are dense and vast; terrestrial reactors must reach over a hundred million kelvin to compensate for their smallness.
