> Book-notes summary of Peter Thiel & Blake Masters, Zero to One (2014).
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the author.

*Zero to One* is Peter Thiel's argument that progress comes in two kinds: **horizontal (1 to n)** — copying, spreading, globalization, adding more of what already works — and **vertical (0 to 1)** — creating something genuinely new, the leap a monopoly or unique innovation makes. Most business advice is about 1-to-n competition; the valuable businesses are built for 0-to-1 monopoly.

The signature question, asked of every interviewee: **"What important truth do very few people agree with you on?"** A good answer has the form "most people believe X, but the truth is not-X." Brilliant thinking is rare, but courage is in shorter supply than genius. Thiel's own answer: most people think the future is defined by globalization; technology matters more.

Core theses:

- **Competition is for losers.** In perfect competition prices are driven to cost and no one makes money. Great businesses are monopolies that capture value by doing something no one else can — "competing means fighting over a shrinking pie; monopolies create their own pie." Monopoly is built from four traits: proprietary technology (ideally 10x better than the closest substitute), network effects (the product grows more valuable with users), economies of scale (marginal costs trend to zero), and branding. Strategy: start with a monopoly share of a tiny market ("be the big fish in a small pond"), then expand adjacent markets — Facebook from Harvard, Amazon from books.
- **Every great company is built on a secret** — an important truth most people don't know or believe they're not allowed to say. Areas people agree are fully explored often hide the best secrets.
- **Definite optimism vs. indefinite optimism.** Thiel maps attitudes toward the future: a definite optimist believes the future can be better and has a specific plan to build it; an indefinite optimist believes the future will improve but doesn't know how (finance, portfolio-ism, consulting). Great startups are definite — a startup is "the largest group of people you can convince of a plan to build a different future."
- **Sales and distribution are as important as product.** "Poor sales rather than bad product is the most common cause of failure." One working distribution channel beats several attempted; superior sales alone can create a monopoly.
- **The seven questions every business must answer:** Engineering (breakthrough, not incremental?), Timing (is now the right time?), Monopoly (big share of a small market?), People (the right team?), Distribution (a way to deliver, not just create?), Durability (defensible in 10–20 years?), Secret (an opportunity others don't see?).
- **Founders and power law.** Venture returns follow a power law — the single best investment in a fund outperforms all others combined; the same concentration applies to careers, choices, and companies. That means maximizing optionality is wrong; finding the one best thing and committing fully is right.
- **Humans + computers.** Thiel argues against both replacement and dismissal: computers are complements, not substitutes for people; the biggest businesses may come from humans and machines together achieving what neither can alone.

The book's contrarianism has critics — the monopoly framing ignores how monopolies are often acquired or regulated away, the political essays are divisive — but as a strategic text its durable content is the inversion it demands: ask where competition is a trap, where small markets are safer than large ones, where consensus is wrong, and where building something *one of a kind* beats competing to be slightly better.
