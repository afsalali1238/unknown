> Reid Hoffman's 2004 LinkedIn Series B deck with his retrospective notes — published at reidhoffman.org.
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the author.

In August 2004, LinkedIn (then 15 months old, 1.2 million users, ~500k monthly) raised its Series B from Greylock, where David Sze invested over internal skepticism. Reid Hoffman later published the original deck with slide-by-slide commentary, making it one of the few canonical venture pitch decks whose author annotated what worked and what he'd change.

Hoffman's core pitch claims and structure:

- **Start with the thesis, not the product.** The deck opens with the network thesis: the internet changes how people and organizations manage professional identity and relationships — professional networks move online just as classifieds, dating, and finance had. Investors are being asked to fund a category change, not a feature.
- **Market and secular trends.** Rise of social networking as a behavior; professionals' need for self-published, searchable identity; recruiter demand for sourcing; the growing web of trust enabling referrals. The deck frames LinkedIn as infrastructure for the "professional graph" — permanent records of work relationships.
- **Metrics that show the network effect working.** User growth, but more importantly engagement and invitation-driven viral acquisition, plus early revenue (job listings, subscriptions) — Hoffman deliberately showed revenue because proving willingness-to-pay undercut the "free audience later" skepticism of consumer networks of the era.
- **Multiple revenue lines tied to one network.** The core insight Hoffman highlights: a professional graph monetizes in several independent ways — subscriptions (power users searching and contacting), hiring (recruiters pay for access), jobs listings, and eventually marketing — and every revenue stream reinforces the value of maintaining a complete profile, which makes the data asset compound. This contrasts with single-revenue marketplaces where monetization conflicts with product.
- **Competition honestly mapped.** At the time Monster and CareerBuilder owned jobs boards, but their data was stale resumes; social incumbents (Friendster/MySpace) owned casual networking but couldn't cross to professionals because of the trust/identity difference.
- **Team and why now.** The founding team's PayPal history (Hoffman, Thiel network) mattered in 2004.

Hoffman's retrospective lessons: **pitch to the model** — know how the investor thinks about the series (market size, moat, monetization, timing) and address each risk; **show the network effect quantitatively** (k-factors, cohorts) rather than asserting it; **prove monetization early but tell the full ambition** — the deck's vision was a platform, but the evidence was job subs; and **"blitzscaling" risk tradeoffs**: raise enough to win the network before well-funded incumbents copied it, even at valuations that looked rich, because networks are winner-take-most. The deck's historical significance is as a template: nearly all marketplace and network pitch decks since borrow its structure (thesis → trend → metrics → monetization wedge → team), and its annotated publication is a primary source for how Series A/B pitches are constructed.
