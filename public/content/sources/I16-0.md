Holden Karnofsky's "The Most Important Century" argues that the twenty-first century is plausibly the most important century in the history of humanity — not by hype but by extrapolation from growth.

The central argument — "This Can't Go On": for almost all of human history the global economy grew at under 0.1% per year. Since the Industrial Revolution it has grown over 2% per year. That pace is not merely unprecedented; it is physically unsustainable on long horizons. Compounding 2% growth for 10,000 years — 5% of the time humans have already existed — would require turning every atom in the galaxy into an economy the size of Earth's. So explosive growth must stop *soon* on historical timescales. The possibilities then form a fork:

- **Plateau/collapse:** growth runs into diminishing returns, regulation, resource limits, or civilizational collapse, and we today live in the unique, tiny sliver of history during which the world rapidly transformed — after which things permanently stagnate or regress.
- **Explosive continuation:** growth keeps going by moving beyond the biosphere and current form of civilization — galaxy-spanning infrastructure, harnessing stars, running minds on hardware (digital people) — reaching a stable, enormous post-human civilization. In that case we are among the first tiny sliver of the quadrillions of beings who will ever exist.
- Either way, **all possible views about humanity's long-run future are wild** — there is no internally coherent "boring future" where 2%-forever continues but nothing strange happens. The future looks *unrecognizable* in any branch.

The mechanism that could drive the explosive branch is **PASTA** — Process for Automating Scientific and Technological Advancement: AI systems capable of conducting the R&D process itself, automating the pipeline that produces new technology including better AI. Karnofsky argues current trends (AI scaling, generalization) make transformative AI plausible within this century, perhaps within decades. If digital people — human-level minds running in software — become possible, the consequences are larger still: population can scale to millions of times its size, minds can run faster, copies can specialize, death becomes optional for those who can afford backing up; the resulting world is stranger than either utopia or dystopia fiction.

The practical upshot is not fatalism or celebration but an action agenda: if the century determines whether civilization permanently plateaus, collapses, or reaches an astronomically large future, then actions that steer the transition — especially AI safety, governance, and institutions capable of handling explosive power — have outsized expected value. Karnofsky frames Open Philanthropy's longtermist strategy around this: take wild scenarios seriously without over-anchoring on any one, evaluate timelines empirically, treat weird ideas as worth investigating, and focus on positive steering of the transition. Critics dispute the timelines, the growth extrapolation, and the focus on far-future scenarios over present suffering — but the framework's core claim, that the present generation may sit at the hinge of history, has become a central reference point in AI-era strategic thinking.
