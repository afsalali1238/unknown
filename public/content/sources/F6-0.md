The Internet protocol suite, commonly known as TCP/IP, is the framework of communication protocols that powers the Internet. Its foundational protocols are the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP), the User Datagram Protocol (UDP), and the Internet Protocol (IP). Early versions were called the Department of Defense Internet Architecture Model because the work was funded by DARPA.

The suite grew out of the ARPANET, the precursor network, whose Interface Message Processors (IMPs) — the first routers — were built by BBN starting in 1969; the first ARPANET message, sent from UCLA to Stanford Research Institute in October 1969, was meant to be "LOGIN" but arrived as "LO" when the SRI host crashed. The ARPANET initially ran a protocol called NCP (Network Control Program), which worked for a single homogeneous network but could not connect networks of different kinds.

The breakthrough came when DARPA began working on packet radio and packet satellite networks in the early 1970s. Bob Kahn, who had helped build the ARPANET, recognized the value of communicating *across* these radically different networks; in the spring of 1973 he began collaborating with Vinton Cerf, then at Stanford, on a next-generation protocol for "internetworking." Their 1974 paper, "A Protocol for Packet Network Intercommunication" (IEEE Transactions on Communications), laid out the design: a gateway (router) between networks, and an end-to-end transport protocol.

Two architectural decisions made the Internet possible. First, the **narrow waist**: IP does the minimum possible job — it addresses packets and routes them across interconnected networks — and assumes the underlying networks are unreliable. The network does not guarantee delivery; it just forwards packets independently, possibly by different routes. Second, the **end-to-end principle**: reliability is the job of the endpoints, not the network. TCP — later split into TCP plus IP — gives the endpoints sequenced, error-checked, retransmitted, flow-controlled communication, while UDP offers plain unguaranteed delivery for applications that prefer speed. Keeping the network dumb and the endpoints smart meant any network technology — wires, radio, satellite, fiber — could join without permission.

The suite is organized into four layers: link (within one network segment), internet (IP, across networks), transport (TCP/UDP, host to host), and application (process to process). The first TCP/IP demonstration linked three networks in 1977; the ARPANET switched from NCP to TCP/IP on a flag day, January 1, 1983. Cerf and Kahn received the Turing Award in 2004, and their design remains one of the most consequential examples in engineering of a minimal, modular, permissionless protocol: the network's simplicity is exactly what let billions of devices and unforeseen applications — the web, streaming, blockchain — be built on top without changing the core.
