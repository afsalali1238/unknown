Inattentional blindness (or perceptual blindness) occurs when an individual fails to perceive an unexpected stimulus that is in plain sight, not because of any defect of vision but because attention is focused elsewhere. When it is impossible to attend to all the stimuli in a situation, a temporary "blindness" can make people miss unexpected — often highly salient — objects and events. The term was chosen by psychologists Arien Mack and Irvin Rock in 1992 and used as the title of their 1998 MIT Press book.

The most famous demonstration is the 1999 study by Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris, "Gorillas in Our Midst: Sustained Inattentional Blindness for Dynamic Events." Participants watched a video of people passing basketballs and were asked to count the passes made by one team — or to count only bounce passes versus aerial passes. Partway through, a person in a gorilla costume walked into the center of the scene, faced the camera, thumped its chest, and walked off — on screen for about nine seconds. Roughly half the participants did not see the gorilla at all. When told afterward, many were incredulous and certain they would never have missed it.

For an event to count as inattentional blindness, four criteria hold: the observer fails to notice an object or event; it is fully visible; they could readily identify it if consciously perceiving it; and the failure is caused by attention being engaged elsewhere in the scene, not by properties of the stimulus itself. The phenomenon is distinct from change blindness (failing to notice changes), repetition blindness, visual masking, and the attentional blink — the distinguishing mark is that the missed stimulus is *unexpected*.

A related phenomenon is cognitive capture or cognitive tunneling: observers too focused on an instrument, a phone, or their own thoughts fail to see the environment — a driver focused on the speedometer or a navigation screen rather than the road. Research has found the effect can occur in anyone, regardless of cognitive ability (though there is evidence it increases with age and may be reduced in some forms of neurodivergence). There is mixed evidence that threatening or reward-associated unexpected objects are noticed more easily.

The lesson generalizes. Seeing is not the passive intake of a picture; it is an active, attention-limited construction, and attention is a single resource with a small budget. Experts — radiologists scanning scans, drivers, pilots, analysts — can miss what they are not looking for even when it sits plainly before them. This is why checklists, peer review, and deliberately re-directed attention matter: the world will put the gorilla on screen, but nothing guarantees that the focused mind will see it.
