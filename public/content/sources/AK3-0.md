*Manufacturing Consent: The Political Economy of the Mass Media* is a 1988 book by Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky. Its title alludes to Walter Lippmann's phrase about the "manufacture of consent" as an art of democracy. The book argues that the mass media of the United States function as a propaganda system: not through state censorship, but through structural economic forces that reliably filter news in directions serving powerful interests.

The centerpiece is the **propaganda model**, which identifies five filters through which news must pass before reaching the public:

1. **Size, ownership, and profit orientation.** The dominant mass media are large, profit-seeking corporations owned by wealthy families or shareholders, or themselves subsidiaries of larger conglomerates. Media that reaches a mass audience requires enormous capital, which selects owners sympathetic to the commercial system; editorial decisions ultimately answer to financial interests.

2. **The advertising license to do business.** Most major media revenue comes not from readers but from advertisers. Advertisers therefore hold "de facto licensing authority": media must attract affluent audiences and avoid content that would alienate sponsors. Working-class and dissident press outlets, unable to compete for advertising, are structurally weakened; editorial choices favor the political and economic tastes of the buyers of ads.

3. **Sourcing mass media news.** Powerful bureaucracies — government, corporations, the military — subsidize the media by providing press releases, briefings, access, and interviews at low cost. They become "routine" trusted sources with privileged access to the gates; dissident sources must struggle for access and can be ignored. A media outlet that falls out of favor with official sources loses information and ultimately audience, so reporting tends to align with official frames.

4. **Flak and the enforcers.** "Flak" means negative responses — complaints, lawsuits, boycotts, legislative pressure, campaigns against the advertisers. Flak is costly to media, and it can be organized by well-funded institutions and think tanks; the threat of it deters the reporting of inconvenient facts and opinions.

5. **A common enemy.** The original fifth filter was anti-communism — an ideology that mobilizes the public against a fuzzy enemy and can brand any dissent as aiding the enemy. Chomsky later argued that after the Cold War this role was filled by the "war on terror." A shared threat legitimizes consensus and marginalizes alternatives.

The model predicts systematic patterns rather than conspiracy: coverage will be favorable to official enemies' victims and skeptical or silent about the crimes of friendly governments; worthy versus unworthy victims get different treatment. Herman and Chomsky supported the model with paired case studies (e.g., coverage of atrocities in Cambodia under Khmer Rouge versus East Timor under Indonesian occupation). The model remains fiercely debated — critics call it unfalsifiable or too crude for today's fragmented media; proponents argue it explains agenda-setting, access journalism, and platform dynamics even more strongly in the era of social media and platform advertising.
