Conway's law describes the link between the communication structure of organizations and the systems they design. It is named after computer scientist Melvin Conway, who introduced the idea in a 1967/68 paper, "How Do Committees Invent?" His original wording was: "Organizations which design systems (in the broad sense used here) are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations."

The reasoning is straightforward. For a system to function, the people designing its parts must communicate with one another to ensure the parts fit together. Where communication is easy and frequent — within a team — components will be tightly coordinated; where communication is hard — across organizational boundaries, between departments or vendors — interfaces will be formal, few, and minimal. The technical structure of the resulting system therefore mirrors the social boundaries of the organization that built it. In colloquial form: complex products end up "shaped like" the org chart that made them.

The law is, strictly, a statement about correspondence rather than cause, though commentators argue about which direction the arrow runs — whether organizational structure dictates technical design, technical design pulls the organization into shape, or both. Evidence has accumulated in its favor: MIT and Harvard Business School researchers testing the "mirroring hypothesis" found strong evidence that loosely coupled organizations produce significantly more modular products than tightly coupled ones, highlighting "the impact of organizational design decisions on the technical structure of the artifacts." Studies at Microsoft and Tampere University of Technology likewise supported the law.

The idea proved practical enough to spawn engineering doctrine. Edward Yourdon and Larry Constantine restated it strongly in *Structured Design* (1979): "The structure of any system designed by an organization is isomorphic to the structure of the organization." James Coplien and Neil Harrison advised agile teams: "If the parts of an organization do not closely reflect the essential parts of the product, or if the relationships between organizations do not reflect the relationships between product parts, then the project will be in trouble... Therefore: make sure the organization is compatible with the product architecture."

The most cited corollary in software is the *inverse Conway maneuver*: rather than let the org chart dictate the architecture, design the organization to produce the architecture you want. Want small independent services? Stand up small independent teams that own them end-to-end. Want a monolith? A single tightly communicating team will produce one naturally. Nigel Bevan captured the user-facing version in 1997: "Organizations often produce web sites with a content and structure which mirrors the internal concerns of the organization rather than the needs of the users."
