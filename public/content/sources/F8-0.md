Moore's law is the observation that the number of transistors in an integrated circuit doubles about every two years, with minimal increase in cost. It is an empirical relationship, not a scientific law — an experience-curve effect measuring efficiency gains from production learning. It is named after Gordon Moore, co-founder of Fairchild Semiconductor and Intel, who in 1965 noted in the magazine *Electronics* that the number of components per integrated circuit had been doubling every year.

The article, "Cramming More Components onto Integrated Circuits," was a speculative commission piece: Moore was asked to predict the next decade of semiconductor components. He observed that component counts had doubled each year since the integrated circuit's invention and, as he later put it, "just did a wild extrapolation saying it's going to continue to double every year for the next 10 years." He speculated that by 1975 a single quarter-inch square of silicon could hold 65,000 components. In 1975 he revised the rate to doubling every two years — a compound annual growth rate of about 41% — and the trend held for decades afterward.

The observation became an industry-wide coordinating target. Rather than merely predicting the future, Moore's law was used to set R&D roadmaps: semiconductor manufacturers, equipment makers, and software companies all planned against the expectation that transistors would get cheaper, faster, and more numerous, and their synchronized investments helped fulfill the prediction. The phenomenon drove falling quality-adjusted prices for microprocessors, the exponential growth of memory and storage, sensor improvement, and even the pixel counts of digital cameras — the technical substrate of the entire digital age.

Related regularities compounded it. Robert Dennard's 1974 scaling observation held that as transistors shrank, their power density stayed roughly constant, so faster chips did not get hotter — giving the industry performance gains essentially for free until about 2005, when leakage and heat walls ended Dennard scaling. Thereafter chips gained cores rather than clock speed.

Moore's law has been declared dead repeatedly — Nvidia's Jensen Huang said in 2022 it was over, while Intel's leadership disagreed — and architects report semiconductor advancement has slowed industry-wide since around 2010, now coming through 3D packaging, specialized accelerators like GPUs, and system-level integration rather than raw transistor density. The larger lesson outlasts the technology: Moore's law is a case study in a prediction that became true because an entire industry organized itself around it, and in exponential growth's compounding logic — roughly a billion-fold improvement in transistor density over fifty years, delivered not by a single breakthrough but by sustained doubling.
