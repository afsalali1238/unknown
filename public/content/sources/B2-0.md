American Research and Development Corporation (ARD) was a venture capital and private equity firm founded in 1946 by Georges Doriot, a professor at Harvard Business School and a general in the U.S. Army, together with Ralph Flanders, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston, and Karl Compton, president of MIT. ARD is generally credited as the first publicly traded venture capital firm and a foundational institution of the modern VC industry.

ARD was founded in the aftermath of World War II to invest in businesses founded by returning soldiers and to commercialize technologies developed during the war. Until then, capital for early-stage companies came mainly from wealthy families and individuals — there was no organized institution for financing new technology enterprises. Doriot's innovation was to treat entrepreneurial finance as a profession: raise institutional money, take equity stakes in young companies, and earn returns when those companies matured.

The firm's historic investment came in 1957. Two MIT engineers, Ken Olsen and Harlan Anderson, pitched building smaller, cheaper interactive computers at a time when investors believed computers were a money-losing business for giants. Rather than fund the computer ambition directly, ARD backed a plan to sell profitable digital circuit modules first: $70,000 in equity for 70 percent of the new company, Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC), plus a loan. When DEC's minicomputers succeeded and the company went public, ARD's stake was worth roughly $355 million by 1971 — a return that made the entire fund and, by demonstration, made the case that venture capital as an asset class could work for institutional money.

Doriot, who became known as "the father of venture capitalism," ran ARD on principles that became the industry's folklore: invest in people more than ideas (he backed " Grade A men with Grade B ideas" over the reverse, he said), stay close to portfolio companies, and accept that the winners, not the average, would drive returns. ARD also invested in companies including Ionics and Tracerlab, and its success helped inspire the formation of later Silicon Valley firms such as Kleiner Perkins and Sequoia — both founded by alumni or close observers of the model ARD pioneered. ARD merged with Textron in 1972, closing the first chapter of institutional venture capital.
