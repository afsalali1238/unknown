Punctuated equilibrium is a theory in evolutionary biology proposing that once a species appears in the fossil record, it remains largely stable — showing little net evolutionary change for most of its geological history. This state of stasis is "punctuated" by rare, geologically rapid events of branching speciation, in which new species arise quickly rather than through slow, uniform transformation of the whole lineage. It was developed by paleontologists Niles Eldredge and Stephen Jay Gould in their landmark 1972 paper "Punctuated Equilibria: An Alternative to Phyletic Gradualism."

The theory began as an observation about the fossil record itself. The Darwinian expectation of *phyletic gradualism* — whole lineages slowly transforming over millions of years, with every intermediate form preserved — is exactly what paleontologists rarely find. Instead, fossils typically appear abruptly, persist unchanged through thick layers of rock, and then disappear, with near-relatives appearing suddenly elsewhere. The conventional explanation was that the fossil record is gappy: sediments preserved only fragments, and the gradual transitions happened in places or times not captured. Eldredge and Gould inverted the argument: treat the stasis and abrupt appearances as real signal rather than missing data, and a different model of speciation explains them.

They built on Ernst Mayr's model of allopatric (especially peripatric) speciation. New species most often form not in the large, stable, central population, but in small, isolated peripheral populations — an island, a valley cut off by climate change — where change can spread fast in a small gene pool, under unusual selection pressures. Because the new species arises rapidly and in a small geographic area, its transitional forms are unlikely to be captured in the fossil record; when it later expands, re-encounters the ancestral range, and persists, it appears in the rocks "suddenly," fully formed, while the ancestral species shows no gradual transition in its home territory. The punctuation is rapid in *geological* time — thousands to tens of thousands of years — even though it is slow by human standards.

The emphasis on stasis was as radical as the punctuations: why do successful species remain unchanged for millions of years despite environmental fluctuation? Eldredge and Gould drew on developmental homeostasis and stabilizing selection — well-adapted, large populations resist change because they are buffered and already near a local optimum.

The theory caused decades of controversy. Critics accused it of anti-Darwinian saltationism or of overselling novelty; Gould insisted punctuated equilibrium was not a rejection of natural selection but a refocusing on the *tempo and mode* of macroevolution, and on species as individuals participating in species-level selection. The empirical debate — how much stasis, how fast the transitions, whether they scale to higher patterns — reshaped paleobiology as a quantitative field. The idea has since traveled well beyond biology: long periods of stability interrupted by rapid change is a recurring pattern in technology, organizations, and history, where established equilibria resist incremental pressure until a peripheral innovation or crisis reorganizes the system quickly.
