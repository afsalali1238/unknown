Grit, as defined by Angela Duckworth and colleagues (2007), is "perseverance and passion for long-term goals" — the capacity to maintain effort and interest over years, through failure and adversity, toward a stable higher-level aim. It is not merely hard work in the moment; it is *directional* consistency, returning to the same goal across years rather than cycling enthusiasms.

Duckworth's research found grit predicted achievement better than IQ in striking settings: grades among Ivy League undergraduates, adult educational attainment, retention at West Point (grit predicted who survived the first summer better than the academy's own admissions composite), and ranking in the National Spelling Bee (gritty kids out-studied peers, especially in deliberate practice). Notably, grit was roughly uncorrelated with IQ — high-achieving and low-achieving-on-talent people can equally have it, which helps explain why some very talented people "underdeliver" and less spectacularly endowed people compound. Grit is closer to the Big Five's conscientiousness than to ability; subsequent work by Marcus Crede and others found most of its predictive power comes from the *perseverance of effort* facet, with "consistency of interests" adding less — effort over years matters more than never changing your mind.

The construct has deep roots: William James in 1907 urged psychology to study how some people access "richer trait reservoirs"; Galton and Aristotle treated persistence as a virtue of achievers. Duckworth's popular book *Grit* (2016) added developmental claims: grit grows through interest (you don't grit yourself into a field you can't care about), practice (sustained, deliberate, feedback-driven effort on weaknesses), purpose (conviction that the work matters to others), and hope (the learned habit of interpreting setbacks as temporary and controllable). She emphasizes that grit is not raw stubbornness — the top-level goal stays fixed while lower-level tactics change — and that parents and culture shape it: adults who are both demanding and supportive, and cultures that normalize long effort and recovery.

Critiques worth knowing: part of the measurement overlap with conscientiousness raises questions of novelty; grit doesn't license praising suffering or telling people to persist at hopeless, structurally blocked paths; and circumstances, resources, and talent remain real — grit is a multiplier on a life trajectory, not a substitute for opportunity. The durable operational content is the time-horizon insight: most ambitious outcomes are won by sustained, same-direction effort over periods where enthusiasm naturally fluctuates. Systems that let people keep going — manageable daily practice, feedback, identity commitments, support through plateaus — outperform both one-off talent and bursts of intensity.
