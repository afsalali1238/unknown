First-principles thinking means breaking a problem down to its most basic truths and reasoning upward from there, rather than reasoning by analogy to what others do. Farnam Street's Julia Child story illustrates the difference.

When Child arrived in France and wanted to learn French cooking, she didn't accept the prevailing rule that cooking genius was inborn and only available to French chefs — she instead deconstructed cooking into principles: how heat behaves with proteins, why emulsions form, what salt does at a molecular level, what each technique (braise, sauté, emulsion) *physically* accomplishes. *Mastering the Art of French Cooking* then taught home cooking from those principles, with exact quantities, failure modes, and reasons — replacing mystical rules with cause-and-effect understanding, so a home cook could adapt and invent instead of follow.

The contrast is reasoning by convention: "this is how it's done" — cookbooks before Child gave dogma (stir this way, this long) without the underlying why. First-principles thinking sacrifices speed for depth: it takes longer up front, but yields transferable mastery (you can fix failures, adapt ingredients, invent) and surfaces improvements invisible to convention — in Child's case, democratizing haute cuisine.

The recurring pattern across famous practitioners — Musk on battery costs (break into commodity inputs: cobalt, nickel, aluminum, housing + yields = far cheaper than the "batteries are expensive" convention), Edison's experimentation, Feynman's physics, Reed Hastings rebuilding entertainment distribution — is the same: identify what is *certainly true* at the foundation and recombine, ignoring the "everyone knows" layer built on assumption. Practice: when stuck, write down what you believe but cannot derive; ask "why must this be true?"; trace any rule to the physics/ economics/ psychology it rests on; then reconstruct. Slow now; portable forever.
