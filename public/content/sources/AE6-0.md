Kishōtenketsu (起承転結) is the four-part structure underlying many classic Chinese, Korean, Japanese, and Vietnamese narratives. In Chinese it is called *qǐchéngzhuǎnhé* (起承轉合), in Korean *giseungjeongyeol* (기승전결), in Vietnamese *khởi thừa chuyển hợp*. The four movements are: introduction, development, twist or reversal, and resolution.

The structure began as a rhetorical form for Chinese poetry. It was described by the Yuan-dynasty scholar Fan Heng (1272–1330) as a method of writing in four styles: *qi* (起), the opening, "straight" and direct; *chéng* (承), the continuation or development, likened to a mortar receiving the pestle — it deepens and supports what the opening set up; *zhuǎn* (轉), the turn, a change of direction that introduces something unexpected; and *hé* (合), the conclusion, likened to a deep pond or overflowing river, where the meaning gathers and reflects.

The feature that distinguishes kishōtenketsu from Western dramatic structure is that conflict is not the engine of the story. Where the three-act structure and the hero's journey organize themselves around an antagonist, escalating obstacles, and a climactic confrontation, kishōtenketsu holds back a major story element until the third act — the *ten* (転) — which often changes the genre or frame of the piece rather than resolving a fight. This is why it has been popularly described, a little misleadingly, as "plot without conflict": stories built on it can contain conflict (Bong Joon-ho's *Parasite* is frequently cited as an example), but they are not *driven* by it the way Western screenplay structure prescribes.

The contrast is useful for writers because it exposes how much Western storytelling treats friction as the only legitimate source of momentum. In kishōtenketsu, the payoff is recognition rather than victory: the *ketsu* (結) brings the opening and the turn together, often with a callback, so the reader re-sees what the beginning already contained. Transitions can be as long as a full paragraph rather than a single line, and the conclusion is meant to be quick — the ending should not linger.
