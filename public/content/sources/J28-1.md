Farnam Street applies mental-model thinking to raising children: the goal is not engineering outcomes but building the decision-making equipment the child will use when you're no longer there.

Key frames: (1) **Incentives matter** — children respond to the reward structures actually in place, not the ones intended; if grades pay, learning that produces learning is deprioritized. (2) **Autonomy and competence** — children develop judgment by making small decisions early and owning small consequences; overprotection removes the learning loop and trains dependency. (3) **Second-order effects** — punishment that stops the visible behavior may teach concealment; help that removes struggle removes the adaptation struggle produces. (4) **Feedback loops and models** — how parents handle their own errors (modeling apology and correction) is the strongest lesson available; children pattern more from what parents do than say. (5) **Trade-offs, not optimization** — every family chooses a portfolio of values (time, money, risk, joy); the goal is conscious trade-offs, not perfection.

The article emphasizes reading, boredom tolerance, and the long game — compounding habits and small daily structures beat dramatic interventions — and treating the parent's job as building the child's process (their mental models) rather than controlling the child's outputs. The same second-order thinking that governs organizational design governs family systems: culture (the home atmosphere and how disagreement is handled) beats rules; alignment between spouses beats policy; and patience with the child's learning curve is the recurring discipline.
