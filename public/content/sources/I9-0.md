*The Sovereign Individual: Mastering the Transition to the Information Age* (James Dale Davidson and William Rees-Mogg, 1997; foreword by Peter Thiel in the 2020 edition) is a libertarian manifesto that became part of Silicon Valley's unofficial canon. Marc Andreessen calls it "the most thought-provoking book on the unfolding nature of the 21st century"; Thiel praises its analysis of the deep forces — "megapolitics" — that determine which societies rise or fall, beyond elections or parliaments.

The book's predictive claims are its fame. Writing on the cusp of the web, it argued that digital technology and cryptography would dissolve the nation-state's monopoly on commerce and coercion: capital and skilled labor would escape taxation and regulation by moving into cyberspace and across jurisdictions; digital currencies would erode state-controlled money; individuals able to operate globally would become "sovereign," negotiating with governments like customers rather than submitting as citizens; populist backlash and inequality politics would rise as the old middle class was hollowed out. Devotees credit it with foreseeing cryptocurrencies, remote work, digital nomadism, populist upheaval, and the competition of states for residents.

The moral-political program is the contested part. Davidson and Rees-Mogg flatter their reader as a "cognitive elite" uniquely positioned to prosper as the old order crumbles — people who "can educate and motivate themselves" will be "free to invent their own work... freed from both the oppression of government and the drags of... prejudice." Inequality is treated as natural; selfish exit — from taxes, from place, from shared institutions — is framed as rationality and even virtue. The book's purpose, stated openly, is to help readers "take advantage of the opportunities of the new age and avoid being destroyed by its impact."

Critics (including academic reviewers and Thiel biographer Max Chafkin) call it less prophecy than a political screed: an ancient fantasy of elites escaping collective responsibility dressed in technological determinism — a technofeudal imagination. The critique gained force as tech billionaires acted literally on it: bunkers, Hawaiian compounds (Zuckerberg's Kauai estate, Benioff's Big Island purchases), citizenship arbitrage (Thiel's fast-tracked New Zealand citizenship despite minimal residence), and seasteading/exit politics. The book's predictions about state power also look overstated: governments proved capable of surveilling, sanctioning, and taxing digital activity; crypto became speculative rather than state-destroying; and the "cognitive elite" still rely on the public systems — property law, security, educated workforces — they imagine outrunning.

The lasting intellectual value, for supporters and critics alike, is the megapolitics frame: that technology shifts the returns to violence, organization, and geography, which in turn reshapes viable institutions. The lasting warning is the ethical inversion — reading historical change as an instruction to insulate rather than contribute.
