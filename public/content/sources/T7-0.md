Sahil Bloom's framework argues that the default scoreboard for a life measures only money, and a broken scoreboard produces broken actions — "what gets measured gets managed." Building a wealthy life means cultivating five distinct forms of wealth, most of which money cannot directly buy and several of which are necessary even to enjoy financial wealth.

1. **Financial wealth** — money and assets. Real but overemphasized; it is *stored optionality*, valuable chiefly as fuel for the other four. It compounds through saving, ownership, and time, and is destroyed by lifestyle inflation and leverage. Bloom stresses financial wealth without the other forms often produces miserable rich people.

2. **Social wealth** — the depth of relationships: close friendships, strong family bonds, community. It is the most robustly evidenced predictor of long-term happiness and health (echoing the Harvard Study of Adult Development). Social wealth requires untransacted, consistent presence; it decays under transactional networking and compounds through attention and vulnerability.

3. **Time wealth** — control over your own calendar and attention: the ability to decide how you spend your days rather than renting them wholesale to obligations. High earners with no autonomy are time-poor; the wealthy point of money is buying back time (outsourcing drudgery, not buying status goods). Time wealth is the rarest because it is wasted automatically by defaults — meetings, commutes, always-on availability.

4. **Physical wealth** — health, energy, and vitality. It operates on a long lag: spent freely in youth, it becomes the binding constraint on everything else later. Sleep, exercise, nutrition, and movement are investments whose returns show up decades on; no amount of the other wealth substitutes for a body that cannot enjoy it.

5. **Emotional (or mental) wealth** — inner stability: low baseline anxiety, resilience, contentment, the ability to be present. It is the platform on which the others are enjoyed; without it money buys worry and time feels empty. Practices include gratitude, limiting comparison and inputs (social media), therapy/meditation, and deliberately defining success for yourself rather than inheriting a template.

The argument's structure is deliberately *anti-correlation-aware*: these forms of wealth can trade off against each other (chasing financial wealth often destroys time, social, and physical wealth), and the strategic question is allocation across all five over a life rather than maximizing one. Bloom's meta-point — live by design, not by default — is that the scoreboard determines behavior: people who only track income optimize income; people who track relationships, autonomy, vitality, and peace actually build those. The practical move is to audit all five periodically (Where am I running a deficit? Which am I borrowing from to feed another?) and to treat the non-financial forms with the same compounding logic as investments: small, consistent deposits, ruin avoided by neglect.
