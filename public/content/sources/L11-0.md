> House-curated overview of logical fallacy education; the TED-Ed collection animates these one at a time.
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading.

Logical fallacies are standard errors in reasoning that render an argument invalid or weak — they *feel* persuasive, which is exactly why they survive. The "demon of reason" collection (and the classic canon of formal logic, critical thinking courses, and cognitive bias research) teaches that bad arguments have recurring shapes. Recognizing those shapes is the fastest improvement in thinking.

The most common:

1. **Ad hominem** — attacking the person instead of the argument ("you're stupid, so you're wrong"). Even a fool can state a truth; an expert can lie.
2. **Straw man** — misrepresenting an opponent's argument into an easier target ("they want open borders" when they proposed one policy change). The fix: steel-man first — state the other side's strongest version.
3. **Slippery slope** — claiming a small first step inevitably cascades to catastrophe, without evidence for the chain. (Some slopes are real; the fallacy is asserting them without the mechanism.)
4. **Appeal to authority** — trusting a claim because of the speaker's position rather than their evidence and field relevance. Experts are shortcuts, not substitutes for reasons.
5. **False dichotomy (false dilemma)** — presenting only two options when more exist ("either the law passes or society collapses").
6. **Hasty generalization** — drawing broad conclusions from small or unrepresentative samples (anecdotal evidence).
7. **Post hoc ergo propter hoc** — because B followed A, A caused B; correlation ≠ causation, and coincidence confuses.
8. **Begging the question (circular reasoning)** — the conclusion appears as a premise ("the Bible is true because the Bible says so").
9. **Appeal to emotion** — manipulating feelings (fear, pity, outrage) rather than proving the point; this *is* most advertising and much political rhetoric.
10. **Red herring / whataboutism** — diverting attention to a tangential or counter-accusation issue.
11. **Bandwagon (argumentum ad populum)** — truth by popularity; most people can be and often have been wrong.
12. **Tu quoque ("you too")** — dismissing a critique because the speaker doesn't follow it. Hypocrisy invalidates neither advice nor fact.
13. **False equivalence** — giving two things equal weight despite vast differences in scale/evidence.
14. **Loaded question** — presupposing the answer ("when did you stop lying?").
15. **The sunk cost / argument from inertia** and **appeal to tradition** — defending a thing because it has been done or already cost.

Why the practice matters beyond debate: these errors are how you fool yourself, not just how others fool you. Motivated reasoning uses them internally to protect identity and prior investments. The practical habits are: name the fallacy type when it appears (the taxonomy is recognition equipment); slow down before conclusions about emotional topics; separate evidence from people and consequences; ask "what would prove this false?"; and treat your own side's rhetoric with the same scrutiny as opponents' — a person who can only spot fallacies in opponents is a debater, not a critical thinker.
