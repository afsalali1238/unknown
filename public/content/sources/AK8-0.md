*The Shallows: What the Internet Is Doing to Our Brains* is a 2010 book by the American journalist Nicholas G. Carr, published by W. W. Norton. It expands the argument of his 2008 *Atlantic* essay "Is Google Making Us Stupid?" and was a finalist for the 2011 Pulitzer Prize in General Nonfiction.

Carr's thesis is that the medium of the internet is not a neutral pipe for the same old reading and thinking; it reshapes the brain doing the reading. Drawing on neuroscience, the history of media, and his own experience of losing the capacity for sustained concentration, he argues that online reading — hyperlinked, interrupted, multimedia, and interactive — trains the brain in rapid, scattered attention, while the deep reading that supports reflection, memory consolidation, and critical thought requires the calmer, linear engagement of the printed page.

The book develops Marshall McLuhan's claim that the medium is the message. Carr surveys earlier transitions — the alphabet, the codex, the printing press, the electric telegraph — arguing that each new medium of information reorganized perception and cognition, not merely its content. The internet's distinctive features are its speed, its interruptions (notifications, links, tabs), and its commercial design for engagement; these features reward skimming, scanning, and rapid switching, and they penalize the undistracted immersion from which complex ideas are built. Carr cites research showing that people reading hypertext and web pages retain less and comprehend less deeply than people reading the same words on paper — the "shallow reading" of the title.

A key move is the appeal to neuroplasticity. The brain is not a fixed instrument that culture writes upon; it rewires itself in response to repeated behavior. Spend years on the web and the pathways of quick scanning, distraction, and reward-by-novelty strengthen, while the slower pathways of contemplation weaken. The worry is not that people become less intelligent but that the cognitive style required for deep work — long-form argument, patience with difficulty, memory for what one read pages ago — atrophies from disuse, even in people who know it is happening.

Carr does not argue for abandoning the internet, whose benefits he grants. He argues for noticing the trade: every medium amplifies certain faculties and starves others, and a society that does most of its reading, thinking, and socializing through a single attention-engineered medium should expect to become collectively shallower, even as it becomes collectively better informed. The book became a foundational reference for later debates about smartphones, social media, notification design, and digital minimalism.
