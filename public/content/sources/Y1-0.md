The Ship of Theseus is the classic puzzle of identity over time. Plutarch recorded it: the Athenians preserved the ship in which Theseus returned from Crete, replacing each decaying plank with new timber as the years passed. Philosophers disputed whether, after every original part had been replaced, it was still the same ship — and if not, at which replacement it ceased to be. Thomas Hobbes sharpened the paradox: suppose someone collected all the discarded planks and reassembled them into a second ship. Which ship is the "real" Ship of Theseus — the continuously maintained one, made of none of the original matter, or the rebuilt one, made of all of it?

The paradox forces two distinct conceptions of identity into the open: sameness of **matter** versus sameness of **form/structure/pattern**. The matter-criterion ship dies the moment the first plank leaves (or becomes partially identical in proportion to shared matter); the form-criterion ship persists through complete material turnover, because its organization and continuity are what matter. Hobbes noted both cannot be the "same numerical ship" without absurdity — two objects identical to one original.

Modern philosophy applies the frame to personal identity. Nearly all the atoms in a human body are replaced over years; yet we consider ourselves the same person. Candidates for what persists: spatiotemporal continuity (the causal chain from past to future self), memory and psychological continuity (Locke's criterion — you are the same person to the extent your consciousness extends back through memory), narrative identity (the self as a story being authored), or functional/organizational continuity. Each view has failure cases: memory is fallible and gappy; teleporters and duplication thought experiments split continuity from uniqueness; split-brain and dementia cases stress the narrative.

The puzzle recurs wherever persistent things are maintained through replacement: a company whose founders, employees, and products are all gone but whose charter and brand persist; software rewritten so many times no original line remains; bands with no original members; cities rebuilt across centuries. Heraclitus's river — you cannot step in twice — is the same issue. The practical lesson is that "same" is not a single predicate: legal identity, material identity, functional identity, and narrative identity answer differently, and many disputes (reboots, renames, migrations, "is this still the same company?") are disputes about which criterion should govern.
