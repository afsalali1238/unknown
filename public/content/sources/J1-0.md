> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the original authors.

Inversion is the discipline of solving problems backward. Instead of asking "How do I succeed?" ask "What would guarantee failure — and how do I avoid it?" Instead of asking how to make a company great, ask what would certainly destroy it (debt, arrogance, bureaucracy, ignoring customers) and then don't do those things. The German mathematician Carl Jacobi, famous for solving hard problems by the maxim *"Man muss immer umkehren"* — "invert, always invert" — would transform a question into its opposite; the answer often became obvious.

Charlie Munger made inversion his signature practical tool. His logic: the world is better understood by what works and what fails, and in many domains avoiding stupidity is easier than engineering brilliance. "Tell me where I'm going to die, so I never go there." Success for individuals and institutions is often the residue of not doing the reliably ruinous things — not marrying the wrong partner, not going broke through leverage, not lying to yourself, not staying in situations where incentives corrupt. Mental arithmetic works this way too: working backward from the answer; investment analysis works this way: imagine the company five years hence bankrupt, then reason backward to what killed it.

Inversion is powerful because forward thinking is dominated by wishful thinking and inside-view optimism; backward thinking accesses better data. Failure modes are concrete, specific, and learnable (from your own failures, but more cheaply from everyone else's). This is why accident postmortems, pre-mortems (imagining a project has failed in the future and writing why), inversion checklists, and "what would be stupid to do?" framing surface risks and errors that planning forward skips. Munger and Buffett's investing is a monument to it: margin of safety, circle of competence, avoiding frauds and permanent loss — a giant "don'ts" list.

Applied deliberately: write down the ways you'd fail at your goal; invert advice into avoidance rules ("do less of what's destructive"); use pre-mortems before launches; prefer options with bounded downside (inverted analysis reveals ruin thresholds — bankruptcy, zero, irreversible harm) and avoid everything that can remove you from the game; and check conclusions by arguing the opposite side as well as you can — Munger refused to hold an opinion until he could state the opposing case better than its proponents. Inversion is not pessimism; it is a complementary algorithm that catches errors forward vision cannot see.
