One of the most common reasoning errors is sliding from **is** to **ought** — treating a description of how things *are* as a prescription for how they *should* be. David Hume identified the is–ought gap: a statement of fact does not by itself contain a moral command; observing that people lie, cheat, or free-ride does not make those actions right.

The error runs in both directions. Descriptive-to-prescriptive: "everyone cheats on their taxes / every company does X" becomes "therefore X is acceptable" — the frequency of a practice is confused with its wisdom, and the base rate of behavior becomes its justification (the prevalence of bad behavior in a system is often evidence the system is broken, not that the behavior is good). Prescriptive-to-descriptive: "rational actors should maximize profit" gets mistaken for an accurate model of how people actually behave — the idealized model becomes the description (in economics this is the critique of Homo economicus; in business, of "the market rewards X" claims where X is what a theory says ought to win).

Discipline for thinking clearly: (1) ask of any claim — is this describing the world (what is true) or endorsing it (what should be done)? (2) remember that "everyone does it" carries no normative weight — popularity and truth are independent; (3) check whether your model of behavior is prescriptive dressed as descriptive (optimization models often are); (4) and run the reverse check too — the way things ought to be is poor evidence for the way they are: assuming other people behave as they should is how fraud and naïveté happen ("why would they lie?").

The frame matters especially in advice: explanations of why a system *currently* works (people respond to incentives the way they do) are not instructions for what you *should* do, and ethical norms often require behaving better than the measured average. The gap between is and ought is where engineering (changing what is toward what ought) lives — treating them as identical makes you either cynical (accepting everything as right) or naïve (expecting ideal behavior).
