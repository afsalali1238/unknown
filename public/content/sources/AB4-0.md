Mendelian inheritance is the type of biological inheritance following the principles Gregor Mendel, a Moravian Augustinian friar, proposed in 1865–66 after years of hybridization experiments on pea plants (*Pisum sativum*) in his monastery garden. Between 1856 and 1863 Mendel cultivated and tested roughly 28,000 pea plants, tracking seven binary traits — seed shape, seed color, flower color, flower position, pod shape, pod color, and stem height — and published his results in the paper *Versuche über Pflanzen-Hybriden* ("Experiments on Plant Hybridization") in 1866.

Mendel found that traits are inherited as discrete units — later named **genes** — rather than blended together, as most nineteenth-century biologists assumed. Cross purebred tall plants with purebred short plants and the first generation (F1) are all tall: nothing blends or averages. But self-pollinate those hybrids and the next generation (F2) returns short plants in a precise 3:1 ratio. Mendel inferred that each organism carries two copies (alleles) of the hereditary factor for each trait, one from each parent; one allele can be dominant (expressed whenever present) and the other recessive (expressed only when both copies are recessive).

From this came two laws. The **law of segregation**: the two alleles for a trait separate during the formation of gametes, so each egg or sperm carries only one allele per trait — which is why recessive traits can skip a generation and reappear. The **law of independent assortment**: different traits (seed color vs. plant height, say) are inherited independently of one another, producing the classic 9:3:3:1 ratios in dihybrid crosses. Mendel's genius was to count ratios across large populations and infer the combinatorial mathematics behind them — biology behaving like probability rather than fluid mixing.

The work was largely ignored for 34 years. It was rediscovered in 1900, independently, by Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns, and Erich von Tschermak, with Correns establishing Mendel's priority. William Bateson vigorously promoted the theory, coining the words "genetics" and "allele." Integrated with the Boveri–Sutton chromosome theory by Thomas Hunt Morgan around 1915 — who showed genes sit linearly on chromosomes, using fruit flies — Mendelism became the core of classical genetics. Ronald Fisher then reconciled Mendel's discrete genes with the continuous variation seen in nature (via many genes acting together) and joined genetics to natural selection in *The Genetical Theory of Natural Selection* (1930), founding population genetics and completing the modern evolutionary synthesis.

The historical lesson is double. Scientifically, Mendel showed that heredity is digital and combinatorial decades before anything physical about genes was known — the ratios demanded the mechanism. Sociologically, a genuinely foundational result can sit invisible for a generation: Mendel's paper was in the literature, cited occasionally, but the scientific community lacked the context to recognize its significance until 1900, when the questions had caught up to the answer.
