Pace layering, developed by Stewart Brand (with Brian Eno, drawing on ecologists C. S. Holling and R. V. O'Neill), explains how durable complex systems — civilizations, ecosystems, organizations — manage to be both adaptive and stable: they are composed of layers that change at *different speeds*, each functionally distinct but each constraining and invigorating its neighbors.

The core dynamic: "Fast learns, slow remembers. Fast proposes, slow disposes. Fast is discontinuous, slow is continuous... Fast and small instructs slow and big by accrued innovation and by occasional revolution. Slow and big controls small and fast by constraint and constancy. **Fast gets all our attention, slow has all the power.**"

The layers of a civilization, from fastest to slowest:

1. **Fashion/art** — froth: quick, experimental, irresponsible; most of it vanishes, but ideas that work sift downward.
2. **Commerce** — fast, opportunistic, innovates and tests; needs the rules of governance and the trust of culture.
3. **Infrastructure** — built once, lasts generations; changes slowly by capital intensity.
4. **Governance** — laws and institutions; must change deliberately; sudden change (the French and Russian revolutions) is catastrophic, while commerce-paced change is too fast for constitutions.
5. **Culture** — language, religion, customs; slow, mostly invisible, supplies deep coherence.
6. **Nature** — the slowest layer; everything above ultimately operates within its constraints.

Health means each layer moves at its own natural pace, supported by slower layers below and kept invigorated by faster ones above. Pathology comes from forcing the wrong pace: commerce pushed to exploit nature at commercial speed destroys fisheries and forests; governance forced to fashion speed becomes unstable and untrustworthy; the Soviet Union forced five-year-plan cadence onto everything, cutting itself off both from fast innovation and from cultural/natural constraints, and was doomed.

The ecological image is a coniferous forest: needles cycle within a year, crowns over years, patches over decades, stands over centuries, the forest over millennia, the biome over ten thousand years. Shock (fire, disease) is absorbed by the fast parts while slow parts maintain continuity. Freeman Dyson frames the same structure in human life through six time scales of survival — individual (years), family (decades), tribe/nation (centuries), culture (millennia), species (tens of millennia), the whole web of life (eons) — which is why human loyalties are inherently layered and conflicting.

For organizations the model maps directly: the daily chat and shipping cadence (fashion/commerce) vs. product architecture (infrastructure) vs. policy and org structure (governance) vs. culture (deep norms) vs. the market/environment (nature). Companies that lock every layer to one pace either become brittle (everything slow, no learning) or chaotic (everything fast, no memory). Resilient systems, in Brand's phrase, "yield as if they were soft" — fast parts absorb shocks so slow parts can keep the system's continuity and identity.
