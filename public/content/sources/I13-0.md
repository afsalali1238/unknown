Progress Studies is the field proposed by Tyler Cowen and Patrick Collison in their July 2019 *Atlantic* essay "We Need a New Science of Progress." Its motivating question: why did material and technological progress speed up in some times and places and stall in others — and how do we get faster, more broadly shared progress now?

Cowen and Collison argue that while many disciplines study pieces of progress (economic history, innovation economics, sociology of science, public health), there is no integrated field asking the practical, teleological question: *what actually makes progress happen faster?* "Progress Studies is closer to medicine than biology: The goal is to treat, not merely to understand." Success would mean identifying effective, progress-increasing interventions and getting them adopted by universities, funders, philanthropists, entrepreneurs, and policymakers.

The empirical puzzle list the field assembles is itself the program: Why did progress slow in construction, infrastructure, and nuclear energy (cost disease, regulatory delays, lost institutional learning) while accelerating in bits? Why did the Great Enrichment happen when and where it did? Why did some cities (Glasgow, Detroit, Shenzhen) become powerhouses and others not? Why did science funding produce better marginal returns in some eras? What made individual inventors and labs so productive, and which institutions (patent prizes, fast trials, permissionless innovation, high-variance funding like Emergent Ventures) select for impact?

By the mid-2020s the movement had institutionalized into a constellation: the Roots of Progress (Jason Crawford), the Institute for Progress, The Progress Network, Works in Progress (which joined Stripe Press), Human Progress at Cato, and Breakthrough Institute's ecomodernism — described by the Progress Network as "building an idea movement that speaks to a better future in a world dominated by voices that suggest a worse one." Cowen administers Emergent Ventures grants, which fund high-upside young individuals and projects rather than conventional programs.

The movement's core claims: progress is real, measurable (health, wealth, energy, knowledge, longevity), historically abnormal, and fragile; stagnation is the default of human history and complacency about growth is dangerous; the binding constraints are often institutional and cultural (speed of permission, tolerance of failure, ambition, coordination) rather than pure resources; and optimism that is action-oriented ("build") beats both declinism and passive techno-fatalism. Critics counter that "progress" needs distributional and environmental qualification, that mid-century technology's speed also produced externalities, and that the field risks ideological selection bias — but its success at re-legitimizing growth and technological ambition as a policy and cultural cause is already its first result.
