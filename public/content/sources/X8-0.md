**Preference falsification** (Timur Kuran, 1987; book *Private Truths, Public Lies*, 1995) is the act of publicly expressing preferences that differ from one's private preferences, under perceived social pressure. It is performative rather than passive — not mere self-censorship (withholding) but actively projecting a contrived preference to shape how others see you. It differs from strategic voting (a private ballot response to electoral math, not social pressure) and from charitable lying (hiding bad news is not concealing a wish). Anonymous surveys often reveal preferences dramatically different from what people voice in public; techniques like list experiments are designed to measure the gap.

Kuran's core machinery: people choose a public preference by balancing two payoffs — the **intrinsic** utility of expressing what they genuinely believe, and the **social** utility of saying what audiences reward. As perceived public opinion shifts, individuals adjust their public positions; crucially, each person's public statement becomes part of the climate that pressures others. The system can therefore settle in a state where a large majority privately opposes a policy, regime, or norm yet publicly supports it — and each person believes others genuinely support it. Kuran calls this a **collective illusion**.

Three consequences follow:

1. **Distortion of social decisions (collective conservatism).** Widely disliked policies persist because reform sympathizers hide their views to protect reputation; each concealed sympathizer makes others feel more isolated, so a private majority coexists with a public majority for the status quo. Secret-ballot elections are the institutional antidote — they decouple voting from social pressure, which is why regimes built on enforced public enthusiasm fear them.
2. **Distortion of private knowledge (knowledge falsification).** Over time people stop merely hiding their beliefs and stop *thinking* them: to reduce cognitive dissonance and the cost of performing views they don't hold, they genuinely adapt their private understanding. Public lies become private truths; societies then collectively forget alternatives, narrowing what is thinkable. This explains how regimes and orthodoxies maintain legitimacy long after their intellectual case has rotted.
3. **Unanticipated social discontinuities.** The same feedback structure that enforces stability can reverse explosively. People hold thresholds (echoing Granovetter): as conditions or small visible acts of dissent shift the perceived climate, a few public defections can make more people safe to defect, cascading suddenly — the regime that seemed eternal collapses in weeks (the Soviet bloc, 1989; sudden falls of entrenched norms). Observers are surprised because the *public* data showed unanimous loyalty; the private distribution was invisible until the cascade.

For everyday contexts the model is immediately usable: meeting silence where everyone privately disagrees but assumes everyone else agrees; corporate yes-culture; virtue signaling and moral panics; ratings and reviews diverging from anonymous sentiment; polls failing where social pressure makes certain opinions costly to state ("shy voter" effects). Kuran's lessons: treat visible consensus as weak evidence about private belief; protect anonymity and channels where private truth can surface (anonymous surveys, blameless postmortems, psychological safety); understand that leaders who force public avowals are buying fragility — compliance that hides dissent is a pile-driver of sudden regime change; and expect that the most rigid-seeming norms are often the closest to a tipping point, because they require the most falsification to sustain.
