The 1986 letter contains one of the most influential pieces of valuation writing in investing: the appendix "Purchase-Price Accounting Adjustments and the 'Cash Flow' Fallacy," where Buffett defines **owner earnings** and explains why the popular "cash flow" figures on Wall Street overstate what owners can actually take home.

Buffett starts with the classic presentation: cash flow = net income plus depreciation, depletion, amortization, and certain other non-cash charges. This number, he says, "customarily is regarded as a measure of... cash that can be used for... capital spending," and sell-side analysts routinely value businesses by this "cash flow." But the calculation leaves out an essential economic reality: capital expenditures. Most businesses must spend, year after year, merely to maintain their competitive position and unit volume — equipment wears out, technology moves, competitors improve. "Why, then," Buffett asks, "are the capital expenditures of a business not charged against earnings? In the case of a fully-owned business, this item is the functional equivalent of depreciation." A business that must spend its depreciation allowance just to stay in place does not produce distributable cash; the non-cash charge was a real expense whose payment was simply deferred into equipment purchases.

The correction is the owner earnings formula:

**Owner earnings = (a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation, depletion, amortization, and certain other non-cash charges, less (c) the average annual capitalized expenditures that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive position and its unit volume** (plus the working capital needs of that position).

Buffett concedes that (c) cannot be precisely audited — unlike GAAC figures, it requires judgment and estimates — but an imprecise but conceptually correct number beats a precise but wrong one. He illustrates with a comparison to his own business: if Berkshire's insurance and operating subsidiaries were sold and bought back, purchase accounting would add amortization charges that distort reported earnings without any cash leaving the building; conversely, sellers of businesses market "cash flow" precisely because it hides the reinvestment burden.

The practical implications endure. First, distinguish businesses where (c) is small relative to (b) — true "consumer monopolies" that need little maintenance capital — from capital-hungry businesses where depreciation understates real costs; the former generate cash owners can deploy elsewhere, the latter merely recycle it. Second, be suspicious of valuation metrics (EBITDA especially, which Buffett derides as treating depreciation as if it were not an expense) that ignore the cost of maintaining the asset base — "does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?" Third, depreciation schedules are accounting artifacts timed by tax rules; economic maintenance spend is what matters, and it may arrive in lumps. Owner earnings is the number Buffett and Munger use to value both wholly owned businesses and marketable stocks — the cash that can be distributed (or reinvested advantageously) without damaging the franchise.
