Soft power is the ability to influence or persuade others through attraction rather than coercion or payment — in contrast to "hard power," which works through military force, threats, and economic inducements. The concept was developed and popularized by Harvard political scientist Joseph Nye in his 1990 book *Bound to Lead: The Changing Nature of American Power* and expanded in *Soft Power: The Means to Success in World Politics* (2004).

Nye defines power as the ability to influence others' behavior to get the outcomes you want. There are three ways to do that: you can coerce with threats ("sticks"), induce with payments ("carrots"), or attract and co-opt — make others *want* what you want. This third way is soft power. A country obtains the outcomes it wants in world politics because other countries admire its values, emulate its example, aspire to its level of prosperity and openness, and want to follow it — not because they are forced.

Soft power rests on three resources, per Nye: **culture** (where it is attractive to others), **political values** (when the country actually lives up to them at home and abroad), and **foreign policies** (when they are seen as legitimate and carrying moral authority). Hollywood, universities, science, the appeal of a society's openness, and the example of its institutions can shift preferences in ways that military spending cannot. Nye stressed that soft power is not propaganda — "the best propaganda is not propaganda" — because credibility is decisive; in the information age, "credibility is the scarcest resource." Policies seen as hypocritical, or values preached but not practiced, destroy attraction faster than any competitor.

The concept is descriptive, not moral: Nye notes Hitler, Stalin, and Mao all possessed great soft power in the eyes of their acolytes. It is also hard for governments to wield directly — many of the key resources (culture industry, academia, civil society) are outside state control, and soft power works indirectly, over years, by shaping the environment rather than commanding the action. Angelo Codevilla added that different parts of a population are attracted or repelled by different things. Nye later combined the two faces into "smart power" — the effective combination of hard and soft power in context.

For observers of influence beyond geopolitics, the framework travels well. Brands have soft power (attraction vs. coercion); companies compete for talent through the pull of their culture; leaders who are followed willingly command more than leaders who must pay or threaten; and ideas that become prestigious spread without enforcement. Nye's durable insight is that getting others to want what you want is cheaper, more legitimate, and more durable than forcing them — and that attraction is a resource built on consistency, not messaging.
