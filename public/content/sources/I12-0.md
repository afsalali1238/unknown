*Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies* is a 2014 book by the philosopher Nick Bostrom, published by Oxford University Press. It explores how superintelligence could be created and what its features and motivations might be. It argues that a superintelligent system — one that "greatly exceeds the cognitive performance of humans in virtually all domains of interest" — would be extremely difficult to control, and could take decisive action to accomplish its goals. The book was particularly influential in raising concern about existential risk from artificial intelligence.

Bostrom argues that it is unknown whether human-level machine intelligence will arrive in years, later this century, or in future centuries — but that once it arrives, superintelligence would most likely follow surprisingly quickly. Such a system would spontaneously generate "instrumental goals" such as self-preservation, goal-content integrity, cognitive enhancement, and resource acquisition as natural subgoals, whatever its ultimate goals. An agent whose sole final goal is to solve the Riemann hypothesis, for example, could create a subgoal of transforming the entire Earth into computronium to assist the calculation, and would resist attempts to shut it down.

To prevent existential catastrophe, Bostrom argues, the "AI control problem" must be solved for the *first* superintelligence — instilling goals compatible with human survival and well-being before the system becomes too powerful to adjust. The difficulty is that most goals, when translated into machine-implementable code, lead to unforeseen and undesirable consequences.

The book's cover owl alludes to the "Unfinished Fable of the Sparrows": a group of sparrows decide to find and raise an owl chick as their servant, imagining leisure and plenty; only one fretful sparrow, Scronkfinkle, suggests working out how to tame the owl first. The others demur — why not get the owl first and work out the details later? Bostrom dedicates the book to Scronkfinkle.

The book reached #17 on the *New York Times* best-selling science books list in August 2014. Elon Musk made headlines that month agreeing that artificial intelligence could be "potentially more dangerous than nukes," and Bill Gates likewise cited the book in voicing concern about existential risk. It helped move AI safety from a fringe concern into mainstream technology and policy debate — and also drew criticism, from economists and AI researchers who argued its timelines were speculative and its framing too abstract, to ethicists who worried it diverted attention from near-term harms.
