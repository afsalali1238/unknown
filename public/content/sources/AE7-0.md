The **narrative paradigm** is Walter Fisher's communication theory (developed through the 1980s): all meaningful human communication occurs through storytelling, and humans are best understood not as rational beings — *homo rationalis* — but as *homo narrans*, storytelling animals. Fisher argued that the prevailing "rational world paradigm," which holds that people are persuaded by logical arguments built on evidence and formal reasoning, fails as a description of how people actually decide. People make sense of the world as a set of stories; they accept stories that match their values and lived experience, and they are persuaded by **good reasons** that come embedded in narrative — shaped by history, culture, character, and emotion — more reliably than by syllogisms.

A story earns belief through **narrative rationality**, which has two components:

- **Coherence (probability):** does the story hang together internally? Are its events and characters consistent? Does it contradict itself or other trusted stories? A coherent narrative has structural flow — beginning, middle, end — and survives comparison with the stories we already live by.
- **Fidelity:** does the story ring true to lived experience? Do its reasons match what we know of human behavior, values, and fact? Fisher says we evaluate fidelity by whether the story's values are appropriate to the decisions it implies, confirmed by our experience, and aligned with the values that make humane action possible.

Fisher's claim is radical in scope: "There is no genre, including technical communication, that is not an episode in the story of life." Scientific papers, legal rulings, and financial reports are narratives too — with protagonists, implicit plots, and evaluative frames — and their persuasiveness depends on narrative coherence as much as formal validity.

This framework is the academic backbone of Jonathan Gottschall's *The Storytelling Animal* (2012), which synthesized research showing that story is the default mode of human cognition: children pretend before they reason; dreams are stories; memory is reconstructed narratively; fiction trains social cognition; and communities and nations cohere around shared origin stories. The practical implications cut across fields: persuasion work is story work (brands, leadership, teaching, politics) — framing facts as a struggle with characters, stakes, and a causal arc moves people where data slides off; critical consumption means asking whose story a "report" tells, which events it foregrounds as plot, and what values its resolution rewards; and self-identity itself is narrative construction — we maintain coherence by editing the story of our past. The warning Fisher and Gottschall both leave: because stories beat arguments, beautiful but false narratives reliably outcompete clumsy truths — making narrative literacy, not just data literacy, the survival skill.
