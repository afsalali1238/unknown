> House-curated overview of the "Wheel of Focus" productivity framework (Suren Samarchyan and popular variants).
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading.

The Wheel of Focus is a weekly-planning framework popularized in productivity discussions (often attributed to Suren Samarchyan) for solving a recurring problem: quarterly and yearly goals don't get done because weeks fill reactively — the urgent crowds out the important. It adapts Eisenhower-style prioritization into a rotation structure.

The mechanics: draw a circle divided into areas of your life/work — e.g., career/current project, health, relationships, finances, learning, side project, fun — typically 5–7 segments. Each Sunday (or planning day), choose **one key result or activity per segment** for the coming week that would make the week a success for that area. The wheel is a checklist that forces (1) explicit allocation of attention to dimensions that would otherwise get none, and (2) a limit — one thing per slice prevents the all-lists-of-40-items failure mode. Each day during the week you schedule the week's wheel items first into calendar slots, before reactively filling remaining time.

The design principles that make it work, drawn from time-management research:

- **Attention as the scarce resource.** Herbert Simon's line — a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention — implies weekly design of where attention goes rather than letting it be auctioned by incoming requests.
- **One thing per area beats many things overall.** Psychologically, small wins (Weick) and concrete next actions (Allen, Getting Things Done) drive execution; a wheel with seven items is completable; a list of forty is guilt furniture.
- **Balanced investment prevents catastrophic borrowing.** Focus wheels explicitly include health and relationships alongside work, formalizing the truth that underweighting them for months creates larger costs than this week's deadline.
- **Weekly granularity is the right horizon.** Long-term goals decompose into weekly increments; days are too small to see balance, months too long to course-correct — the week is the unit most controllable and closest to results.

Usage variants: productivity versions map segments to roles (Covey's roles-as-segments idea from The 7 Habits); team versions (manager wheels) assign segments to projects/people; some practitioners pair the wheel with MITs (most important tasks) — three daily MITs derived from the wheel's segments. Common failure modes: putting aspirational rather than completable items ("get fit" vs. "exercise three times"), not calendar-blocking, and treating uncompleted segments as moral failures rather than diagnostic data for next week's design. The meta-lesson behind the wheel and most durable productivity systems: you cannot manage time (it passes regardless); you design defaults and review the gap between intended and actual allocation, weekly, forever.
