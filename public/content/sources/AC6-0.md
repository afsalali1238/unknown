*The Fractal Geometry of Nature* is a 1982 book by the Franco-American mathematician Benoît Mandelbrot, published by W. H. Freeman and Company. It is a revised and enlarged version of his 1977 book *Fractals: Form, Chance and Dimension*, itself a revised and translated version of his 1975 French book *Les Objets Fractals: Forme, Hasard et Dimension*. *American Scientist* placed the book among its one hundred books of twentieth-century science.

The book argued that the rough, irregular shapes of nature — coastlines, mountains, clouds, trees, rivers, and lungs — are not mathematical nuisances to be smoothed over but the signal. Mandelbrot coined the term *fractal* (from the Latin *fractus*, broken or fractured) for shapes that display self-similarity across scales: zoom in on a coastline and you see bays within bays, on a branch and you see twigs within twigs.

Mandelbrot's central mathematical object, later named the Mandelbrot set, is defined by a deceptively simple rule: take a complex number *c*, repeatedly square it and add it back; if the result stays bounded, *c* belongs to the set. The boundary of that set turns out to be infinitely complex — magnify any stretch and new structures appear forever, with the whole shape recurring inside itself. The set's boundary is "the most complex object in mathematics," yet it is generated by a formula a child can compute.

The book's influence depended heavily on its pictures. Working with programmers at IBM Research, Mandelbrot produced computer-generated visualizations of fractal forms that looked eerily like real landscapes and organisms. These images carried the argument where equations alone could not: that simple recursive rules can generate the tangled, irregular complexity of the natural world. Fractal geometry went on to underwrite fields from computer graphics and antenna design to the study of heartbeats, financial markets, and networks — and it shaped the intuition that self-similar, scale-free structure is a signature of complex systems everywhere.
