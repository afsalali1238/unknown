The **illusion of transparency** is the tendency to overestimate how much our internal state — thoughts, feelings, intentions — is evident to others. We speak as if our minds were visible, assuming our hints were taken, our emails were clear, our discomfort was shared; observers instead see only behavior and infer their own stories.

Research backing: the spotlight effect studies (Gilovich) found people think others notice them roughly twice as much as they do — wearing an embarrassing T-shirt, a third fewer peers noticed than subjects predicted. In experiments where tappers tapped familiar melodies for listeners to identify, tappers estimated ~50% success; listeners guessed ~2.5% — the tapper *hears* the full song internally and cannot stop the knowledge leaking into their estimate of the listener's experience (a version of the curse of knowledge).

Consequences: presenters imagine audiences can follow half-finished arguments; spouses feel uncared-for because their unstated expectations go unmet; negotiators believe their red lines are obvious; managers assume their worries were communicated. The cost is avoidable miscommunication and resentment on both sides.

Countermeasures: over-communicate explicitly — say the conclusion, state the context, write the meeting's purpose; ask people to paraphrase back (the real test of clarity is the listener's understanding, not your intention); never treat silence as agreement; remember that the other person lacks your information and feels equally obvious to themselves. The same principle runs in reverse: you cannot read others' minds either, so ask rather than infer.
