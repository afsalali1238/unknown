> David Foster Wallace's 2005 Kenyon College commencement address, archived via Farnam Street.
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the author.

David Foster Wallace's *This Is Water* opens with two young fish swimming along, who meet an older fish who says "Morning, boys. How's the water?" The young fish swim on, then eventually ask: "What the hell is water?" The point: the most obvious, important realities are the ones hardest to see and talk about.

A liberal arts education, Wallace argues, is less about knowledge than about **what and how you choose to think**. The default state of an adult life — traffic, grocery lines, tedious work, being tired and bored and surrounded by people who may be annoying — generates automatic self-centered interpretations: I am the center of the world, and these other people are obstacles to my immediate comfort. That "default setting" is the water: invisible, automatic, and it treats your own wants and frustrations as real while other people's inner lives are invisible.

The alternative — the freedom a real education is for — is not about virtue or abstract high-mindedness. It is the **ability to choose what to pay attention to**, to construct meaning by thinking about what you are experiencing differently: the person in the SUV cutting you off may have a medical emergency; the slow cashier may be getting through a terrible day. The point is not that the generous interpretation is always right; it's that you get to decide, with attention and discipline, how to interpret a situation you cannot fully know.

Wallace warns against mistaking the speech for a moral lecture or religious truth: the claim is that awareness itself — noticing that you are operating on automatic — is the difference between a conscious and an unconscious life. The world does not present itself meaning-ready; you construct meaning with what you attend to. And that is hard, because self-centered attention is the default, because adult life is largely devoid of speeches that remind you, and because the truths that matter ("this is water") are the ones that become cliché precisely because they are true and because they are the first thing we forget.

The speech's place in decision-making education: it is the attention-economy argument in literary form — control of attention is control of experience; and it frames the entire mental-models project: learning to step outside your default frame is learnable, unglamorous, and the most important adult skill.
