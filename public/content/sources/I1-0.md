> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the authors.

Chris Dixon's "toy theory" explains why transformative technologies routinely sneak by the companies that should have owned them: **the next big thing always starts out being dismissed as a toy**. The framework comes from Clay Christensen's disruptive innovation theory, built on one observation: technologies improve faster than users' needs increase. Incumbents rationally optimize for their current, demanding customers; a new entrant that is worse on the established metrics — flimsy, simple, cheap, hobbyist-grade — looks inferior and safely irrelevant. By the time the new technology has improved enough to eat the incumbent's market, the incumbent's skills, business model, and culture are aligned to the old trajectory.

Dixon's examples are the canonical history: the telephone was dismissed as a toy compared with the serious telegraph (for serious business communication!); PCs were dismissed as toys by minicomputer makers (Digital Research's executives couldn't imagine anyone wanting a computer at home); the early internet was dismissed by AOL/Prodigy-era media as a niche for nerds; mobile was dismissed by desktop web companies; "stupid apps" (Twitter, Foursquare, then TikTok-era social) were dismissed despite becoming the defining platforms of their decade. Sam Altman's companion post, "Stupid Apps and Changing the World," gives the strategic advice: there are two tested ways to change the world with technology — build something some people love and most people think is a toy, or be hyper-ambitious (electric cars, rockets); and don't claim you're changing the world until you have.

Why "toys" win, structurally: they can iterate without scrutiny — incumbents don't defend markets they don't see; they attract underserved or new users (young people, hobbyists) whose needs are not met by overbuilt products; they can grow under the radar until improvement curves cross; and they are built by people optimizing for love of a small audience rather than approval of the mainstream.

The corollaries for entrepreneurs and investors: a product that looks unserious but has passionate early users is the classic disruption signature; being dismissed is protective; the first version should embarrass you (Reid Hoffman), the MVP should collect maximum validated learning with least effort (Ries), and "the perfect is the enemy of the good" (Voltaire) is the same insight across centuries. For incumbents, the lesson is the hardest: your best customers' satisfaction is a lagging indicator; the threats arrive in forms your evaluation rubrics score worst. When a new technology seems too trivial to matter — especially to your expert judgment — that is exactly the moment Christensen, Dixon, and the historical record say to look closer.
