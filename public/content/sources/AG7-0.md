*General Systemantics* (retitled *Systemantics* in its second edition and *The Systems Bible* in its third) is a treatise by the pediatrician and systems thinker John Gall, first published in 1977 after thirty publishers rejected it and it was self-published. Written with dry humor, it offers practical principles of systems design drawn from experience and anecdotes — mostly from the perspective of how *not* to design systems.

The book's primary precept is that large complex systems are extremely difficult to design correctly despite the best intentions. The lesson is therefore to design smaller, less complex systems and to build them incrementally, in close and continual touch with user needs and measures of effectiveness.

Gall's premises are deliberately blunt. "Systems in general work poorly or not at all." He traces this observation through Murphy's Law ("if anything can go wrong, it will"), Korzybski's general semantics (which blamed failure on bad communication), and Parkinson and Peter's laws of bureaucratic behavior — Parkinson's Law that work expands to fill the time available, and the Peter Principle that people rise to their level of incompetence. Gall then states his own first principles:

**New systems mean new problems.** Once a system is set up to solve a problem, the system itself generates new problems of development, operation, and maintenance. The additional energy required to support a system can consume the energy it was meant to save.

**Systems tend to expand to fill the known universe.** A system becomes an entity in its own right: it not only persists but grows, encroaching into areas beyond its original purpose.

**Complicated systems produce unexpected outcomes** — Gall's "Generalized Uncertainty Principle." He cites spectacular cases: the Aswan Dam diverted the Nile's fertilizing sediment into Lake Nasser, where it was useless, and ended up feeding a snail-borne disease; urban renewal projects designed to reduce slums often created worse ones. The behavior of large systems cannot be fully predicted from their designs.

The title is a joke on Korzybski's "general semantics": where Korzybski thought all system failure traced to a single root cause (failure to communicate), Gall observes that failure is an *intrinsic* feature of systems themselves — "general system antics." Systems, in other words, naturally act up.

The book became a cult classic in software engineering and design; its most-quoted line — "A complex system that works is invariably found to have evolved from a simple system that worked. A complex system designed from scratch never works and cannot be patched up to make it work" — is often cited as a founding text of the philosophy of building simple, composable systems.
