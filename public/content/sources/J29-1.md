Most people stop at the first consequence of a decision. **Second-order thinking** (Howard Marks calls it second-level thinking in *The Most Important Thing*) asks: "And then what?" — pushing through the second, third, and nth-order effects.

First-order thinking is fast, easy, and nearly universal: "the outlook is favorable, stock goes up." Everyone reaches the same conclusion, so no edge is possible. Second-order thinking is deliberate and complex — thinking in terms of interactions, time, and system response. The classic failures are immediate-benefit actions whose later effects dominate: funding "moderate rebels" who become decade-long enemies; eating sugar for energy that produces the afternoon crash and long-term weight; rent control giving housing relief in year one and shrinking the housing supply in year ten. Ray Dalio: "Failing to consider second- and third-order consequences is the cause of a lot of painfully bad decisions, and it is especially deadly when the first inferior option confirms your own biases."

Practices: (1) always ask "and then what?"; (2) think across time — consequences at ten minutes, ten months, ten years; (3) write out 1st/2nd/3rd-order consequence chains to calibrate; (4) for business decisions, map ecosystem responses — employees, competitors, suppliers, regulators. Many of the best outcomes are first-order negative, second-order positive (exercise, honesty, delayed gratification), which is why fewer people pursue them: first-order thinkers avoid the near-term cost and never reach the payoff.
