Tit for tat is an English saying meaning "equivalent retaliation," first recorded in 1558. It is also a highly effective strategy in game theory: an agent using it cooperates on the first move, then on every subsequent move replicates whatever the opponent did on the previous move. If the opponent cooperated, the agent cooperates; if the opponent defected, the agent defects in return. The strategy resembles reciprocal altruism in biology.

Tit-for-tat became famous through Robert Axelrod's iterated prisoner's dilemma tournaments at the University of Michigan around 1980. Game theorists from around the world submitted computer programs, each encoding a strategy for playing repeated prisoner's dilemma against all comers; the winner was the strategy with the highest total payoff. On both occasions, the winner was submitted by mathematical psychologist Anatol Rapoport — and it was the shortest program in the tournament: just a few lines implementing tit for tat. It was simultaneously the simplest strategy and the most successful, beating elaborate attempts to exploit opponents.

The surprise was that a largely cooperative strategy won so decisively. Theorists had long known that in a *one-shot* prisoner's dilemma defection is the dominant strategy — but the tournaments model life, in which you face the same people repeatedly. Under iteration, a strategy must survive play against itself (many competing strategies cooperate, so a program that reflexively defects loses the gains of mutual cooperation) while not letting exploiters walk all over it. Tit for tat's success was analyzed into four properties:

It is **nice** — it begins by cooperating and is never the first to defect, so it never leaves gains from mutual cooperation on the table. It is **provocable** — it retaliates immediately when the other side defects, so exploiters cannot take advantage for free. It is **forgiving** — after retaliating once, it returns to cooperation as soon as the opponent does, so a single misunderstanding does not lock both sides into endless defection. And it is **clear** — its rule is so simple that opponents quickly learn what to expect, which makes cooperation with it predictable and easy.

Axelrod's book *The Evolution of Cooperation* (1984) generalized the result to explain how cooperation can emerge among self-interested parties without friendship, foresight, or central authority — among soldiers in World War I trench "live and let live" arrangements, among bacteria, and in international politics. A key condition is the shadow of the future: parties must expect to meet again, and care enough about future payoffs, for retaliation and reciprocity to discipline behavior. Later research found that "tit for tat with forgiveness" — occasionally cooperating after a defection to escape vendettas caused by noise — performs even better in error-prone environments.
