Will and Ariel Durant's *The Lessons of History* distills millennia into broad patterns. The Farnam Street "three buckets" framing asks what history can and cannot teach, sorting its uses:

1. **Things that change** — the surface: technology, fashions, empires, institutions, vocabulary. Specifics rarely repeat; predicting from them is prediction by analogy and fails often.
2. **Things that recur** — human dynamics across similar structures: greed and fear in markets, overextension of powers, the cycle of debt, war and revolution after inequality, the corruption of concentrated power, generational forgetting. These recur because they arise from stable human nature under stable constraints — power law dominance, limited attention, in-group loyalty.
3. **Things that never change** — the physical and biological substrate: scarcity, time, death, status desire, learning curves, compounding, thermodynamics. Rules at this layer are essentially permanent in the human era.

The Durants' own theses: progress in knowledge has not much changed emotional life (we have more power, not necessarily more virtue); inequality concentrates in stable societies until a tax/rebellion/collapse cycle resets it; societies grow rich and then spend, preferring consumption to production, inviting competition; religion and moral consensus hold civilizations through hardship and weaken with affluence.

The practical method: when evaluating a "new" phenomenon (AI hype, crypto, geopolitics, a startup), ask which bucket the relevant variables are in — the technology changes, but mania dynamics, incentives, and attention scarcity do not. Old books survive because they describe the bottom two buckets; news describes the top bucket and is mostly noise. Reading history for analogies means *patterns, not events*: match structure (credit bubbles, overreaching empires, platform lock-ins) rather than names.
