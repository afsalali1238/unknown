**Survivorship bias** is the error of studying only the survivors and concluding their traits explain success, while the failures — which share or lack the same traits — remain invisible.

The canonical story is Abraham Wald and WWII bomber armor: the military mapped bullet holes on returning planes and proposed reinforcing the most-hit areas (fuselage, wings). Wald, of the Statistical Research Group, argued the opposite — armor the spots with *no* holes, the engines and cockpit. Those are the planes that never came back; the holes on the surviving planes mark damage survivable. The sample was the survivors; the dead data was missing.

Other examples: "old buildings were built better" — the demolitions are gone; "successful founders dropped out of college" — the failures of dropouts aren't profiled (and successful graduates doing well rarely cite their degree as the reason); fund performance ads exclude closed funds; shark-strike statistics are dominated by survivors who report; advice from winners on habits conflates common practices of winners with what made them win.

Fixes: when analyzing success, explicitly ask what happened to the failures (seek *necrological* data, closed companies, dropped-out people, dissolved relationships); compute base rates across the whole population before extracting lessons from exemplars; treat "X did Y and won" as near-worthless without "how many who did Y lost"; and remember the returns from copying a survivor habit in a lottery are the population average, not the survivor's result.
