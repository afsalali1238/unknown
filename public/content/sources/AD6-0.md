The amygdala is a paired nuclear complex in the temporal lobes, considered part of the limbic system. Thirteen nuclei have been identified within it, the chief ones being the basolateral complex (lateral, basal, and accessory basal nuclei), the central nucleus, the cortical and medial nuclei, and the intercalated cell clusters. The basolateral complex connects extensively with higher-order cortical areas — prefrontal, temporal, and insular cortex, and the hippocampus — while the central nucleus projects down to the brainstem and hypothalamus. The amygdala plays a primary role in the processing of memory, decision-making, and emotional responses, including fear, anxiety, and aggression.

Joseph LeDoux's 1996 book *The Emotional Brain* synthesized his laboratory's work on how fear is routed, and it became the canonical popular account of the amygdala's role. LeDoux showed that sensory information about a potential threat reaches the amygdala along two parallel paths. The "low road" runs from the thalamus directly to the amygdala — fast, crude, and operating before consciousness. The "high road" runs from the thalamus through the sensory cortex, where the stimulus is identified and evaluated properly, and only then to the amygdala — slower, but accurate.

The low road is why you can jump at a stick on a path before you have consciously registered that it is a stick: the thalamus forwards a rough "possibly snake" signal to the amygdala, which triggers the body's fear response within about twelve milliseconds, while the cortical analysis that identifies the object takes closer to thirty. LeDoux called this "pre-attentive" processing; the amygdala, he argued, acts as a kind of alarm that does not wait for the thinking brain to finish its work. Walter Cannon had described the resulting bodily mobilization as the fight-or-flight response as early as 1915 — the sympathetic nervous system dumps adrenaline, the heart races, attention narrows, digestion shuts down — but LeDoux mapped the neural wiring that decides to trigger it before the conscious mind has a view.

The lateral amygdala is the main entry point for sensory information and the site where fear memories are learned; the central amygdala orchestrates the responses — freezing, blood-pressure changes, stress-hormone release. LeDoux later refined the framework, arguing in *Anxious* (2015) that the amygdala is better understood as a threat-response circuit than as the seat of the conscious *feeling* of fear, which he attributed to cortical processes. But the core finding stands: a great deal of emotional behavior is generated subcortically, ahead of reasoned perception — which is why deliberate emotional regulation is hard, and why exposure-based therapies work by slowly retraining the amygdala's learned associations rather than by argument.
