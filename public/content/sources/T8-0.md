SMART goals (George T. Doran, 1981) ask that goals be Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Timely. They work fine for small, short-term, well-understood targets. Anne-Laure Le Cunff argues they are the wrong tool for ambitious, long-term endeavors — learning to code, writing a book, building a newsletter, changing careers — where you cannot know the timeline, the end-state is unclear at the start, and judging "achievable" in advance means deliberately aiming low. A goal like "become fluent" or "grow an audience" cannot honestly be specified, measured, or time-boxed in advance.

Her alternative is a **PACT** — designed around output (what you do) rather than outcome (what results):

- **Purposeful.** The goal must connect to your long-term purpose, not merely current relevance. Ambitious goals are sustained through months of unrewarded work by meaning, not metrics. (Routine chores needn't be purposeful — they're tasks, not goals.)
- **Actionable.** Based on outputs you control: shift from future outcomes ("get 5,000 subscribers") to present actions you can take today ("publish a newsletter each week"). You control effort and process; you don't directly control market response.
- **Continuous.** Simple, repeatable actions that compound — daily practice, weekly publication. Rigid end-goals encourage choice paralysis from over-planning; continuous goals are flexible: start before you know the final form, adapt as you learn, pursue improvement rather than a finish line.
- **Trackable.** Not "measurable" in the analytics sense — trackable with the simplest possible ledger: yes/no, like a GitHub contribution graph. Did you code today? Did you call three customers? Did you publish? Binary tracking is honest, frictionless, and hard to game.

The contrast in her example: the SMART version of a newsletter goal is "Get 5,000 subscribers in 25 weeks" — a target on variables largely outside your control, which invites discouragement when subscriber growth doesn't comply. The PACT version is "Publish 25 newsletters over 25 weeks" — fully controllable, repeatable, and the growth becomes a probable side effect rather than the pass/fail criterion.

The deeper principle is one of control and identity: in uncertain, long-horizon domains, process goals beat performance goals because you can guarantee the inputs, build compounding habits, and keep feedback local and fast. It connects to systems-over-goals thinking (Clear's identity habits, Seneca's focus on effort not outcome), and to the ageless advice that in creative and learning domains, showing up repeatedly is the only reliable strategy — make the promise about what you'll do, not about what the world will hand you.
