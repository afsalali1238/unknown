In the 1997 letter, Buffett reports a 34.1% gain in per-share book value — and immediately refuses to take a bow. "Any investor can chalk up large returns when stocks soar, as they did in 1997. In a bull market, one must avoid the error of the preening duck that quacks boastfully after a torrential rainstorm, thinking that its paddling skills have caused it to rise in the world. A right-thinking duck would instead compare its position after the downpour to that of the other ducks on the pond." Berkshire's duck rating for the year: "Quack." The proper benchmark is the S&P index over time, net of Berkshire's corporate-tax drag; the goal is only "a modest advantage over the Index."

The letter states a math of size that every investor should internalize. In 1951, in Ben Graham's class at Columbia, an idea giving Buffett a $10,000 gain improved his year by 100 percentage points. In 1997, an idea producing $500 million of pre-tax profit added *one percentage point* to Berkshire's results. "For anyone deploying capital, nothing recedes like success... We weren't smarter then, just smaller. At our present size, any performance superiority we achieve will be minor."

With prices high for both businesses and stocks — "we get relatively little in prospective earnings when we commit fresh money" — Buffett turns to Ted Williams. In *The Science of Hitting*, Williams divided the strike zone into 77 cells, each the size of a baseball. He only swung at cells where his batting average was high; waiting out the bad pitches was the whole secret. Investing is harder in one way: nobody calls a strike. You can stand at the plate forever and nothing happens; no umpire penalizes you for not swinging. That is the advantage — **you can wait for the fat pitch**. "Waiting for the fat pitch" becomes Berkshire's discipline: hold cash, refuse forced action, and bet big when an easy, high-confidence opportunity arrives.

The broader lessons: distinguish luck from skill by comparing to the right benchmark (the other ducks, not the rainwater); recognize that compounding at scale inevitably slows, so expectations must shrink with capital; and understand that in investing, unlike baseball, you are never forced to swing. Activity is not a virtue; patience is a structural edge. The letter also previews the owner-earnings framework developed in the 1986 letter — intrinsic business value, not book value or reported earnings, is what counts — and celebrates managers "motivated by the joy of accomplishment, not by fame or fortune."
