*The Seven Basic Plots: Why We Tell Stories* is a 2004 book by the British journalist and author Christopher Booker, the product of thirty-four years of work. It offers a Jungian analysis of stories, arguing that beneath the surface variety of the world's myths, novels, films, and plays lies a small set of recurring shapes that map onto deep psychological patterns.

All successful stories, Booker argues, follow an underlying "meta-plot" with five stages. The *anticipation stage* summons the hero toward adventure. The *dream stage* brings initial success and an illusion of invincibility. The *frustration stage* brings the first confrontations with the enemy and shatters that illusion. The *nightmare stage* is the climax, where hope seems lost. Finally the *resolution* brings triumph over adversity against the odds. "However many characters may appear in a story," Booker writes, "its real concern is with just one: its hero... What each of the other characters represents is really only some aspect of the inner state of the hero himself."

Over that arc Booker lays the seven plots:

- **Overcoming the Monster** — the hero sets out to defeat an antagonistic force threatening the homeland (Beowulf, Dracula, *Jaws*, *Star Wars: A New Hope*).
- **Rags to Riches** — the poor protagonist gains power, wealth, or a partner, loses it, and gains it back, having grown (Cinderella, *Great Expectations*, "The Ugly Duckling").
- **The Quest** — the hero and companions set out to acquire an object or reach a place, facing temptations and obstacles (the *Iliad*, *The Lord of the Rings*, *Raiders of the Lost Ark*).
- **Voyage and Return** — the protagonist goes to a strange land and returns changed (Gulliver's Travels, *The Wizard of Oz*, *Alice in Wonderland*).
- **Comedy** — confusion and mistaken identity resolve into social harmony, usually marriage (Shakespeare's comedies).
- **Tragedy** — the protagonist's flaw drives them to a dark end (*Macbeth*, *Doctor Faustus*).
- **Rebirth** — the protagonist falls under a dark power and is redeemed by love or grace (*A Christmas Carol*, *Sleeping Beauty*).

Booker's thesis is that these shapes persist because story is a kind of psychological machinery: the hero's movement from constriction to integration mirrors the process by which the self becomes whole. The book attracted criticism for the rigidity of its taxonomy and for dismissing many celebrated modern works as failures to follow the meta-plot, but it remains one of the most comprehensive attempts ever made to catalogue the grammar of narrative.
