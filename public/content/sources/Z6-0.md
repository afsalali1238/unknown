Prime editing, developed by David Liu's lab (Anzalone et al., *Nature*, 2019), is a "search-and-replace" genome editing technology that directly writes new genetic information into a targeted DNA site without the double-strand breaks that CRISPR-Cas9 relies on.

The system has three core components:

1. **A prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA).** An extended guide RNA that both identifies the target site and carries the template for the new sequence. It contains a primer binding site (PBS), which lets the nicked DNA strand hybridize to the pegRNA, and a reverse transcriptase template encoding the desired edit.
2. **A Cas9 nickase.** A modified Cas9 (typically the H840A variant) that cuts only one DNA strand — making a single-strand nick instead of a double-strand break. Double-strand breaks trigger error-prone repair and indels; the nick avoids most of that collateral damage.
3. **A reverse transcriptase.** Fused to the nickase; it reads the RNA template and synthesizes the new DNA sequence directly into the nicked strand. The cell's repair machinery then integrates the edited sequence and resolves the unedited strand to match.

The crucial advantages over earlier tools: CRISPR-Cas9 cuts both strands and depends on the cell's homology-directed repair, which is efficient mainly in dividing cells and produces uncontrolled mixtures of edits; base editors can convert only one base to a few others (C→T, A→G) within a small window. Prime editing can in principle mediate all twelve base-to-base conversions, plus targeted insertions and deletions, with fewer off-target effects and no donor DNA template — vastly expanding the range of genetic variants that can be cleanly written. Since the vast majority of known disease-causing mutations are point mutations or small indels, the technology covers a large fraction of the genetic disease space (early estimates suggested ~89% of known pathogenic variants could in principle be addressed by prime editing).

As of recent years prime editing has been demonstrated in animal models of genetic disease, in plants, and in bacteria; the first human treatments were reported in 2024, with chronic granulomatous disease patients effectively cured in early trials. Delivery to the right tissues, efficiency in non-dividing cells, and safety profiling remain active engineering problems. Conceptually, prime editing marks a shift in biological capability from cut-and-hope editing (induce breaks, let repair happen) to literal word-processing of the genome — searching for a string and replacing it with an authored string — a qualitatively tighter form of control over the code of life, with the corresponding questions about off-target effects, germline use, and equitable access.
