The arrow of time is the one-way asymmetry of time: the difference between past and future that makes a video of a shattering glass look absurd in reverse. Arthur Eddington named and popularized the concept in *The Nature of the Physical World* (1928). He gave a rule: follow an arrow drawn in time; if randomness (entropy) increases along it, it points to the future; if it decreases, to the past. "The introduction of randomness is the only thing which cannot be undone."

The paradox: at the microscopic level the fundamental laws of physics are almost entirely time-symmetric. Equations governing individual particles work equally well forward and backward; a film of two colliding molecules looks plausible either way. Yet at the macroscopic level irreversibility is everywhere — heat flows hot to cold, ice melts, cups break, memories accumulate. The resolution lies in statistical mechanics, via Boltzmann: a macrostate corresponds to an enormous number of microstates, and disordered states vastly outnumber ordered ones. Starting in a low-entropy state, a system evolving randomly will almost certainly move toward higher entropy — not because any particle is directed that way, but because there are overwhelmingly more ways to be disordered. Entropy increase is a probabilistic fact about coarse-grained descriptions, not a force in the microscopic laws.

This explains the connected arrows of experience: the **thermodynamic arrow** (entropy increase) grounds the **psychological arrow** (we remember the past, not the future — memory formation creates stable low-entropy records, which requires net entropy export) and the **causal arrow** (causes precede effects). Eddington stressed the arrow appears in physical science "only in the study of organization of a number of individuals" — it is a statistical, emergent property, absent from any single particle.

The deep open question is why entropy was so low in the past: the second law predicts the future given a low-entropy boundary, and that boundary appears to be the Big Bang. Sean Carroll's framing: time symmetry in the laws is like spatial isotropy — laws treat all directions alike, but near Earth "up/down" is distinguished by proximity to a massive body; near the Big Bang, "past/future" is distinguished by proximity to the special low-entropy state. The irreversibility that shapes everything practical — aging, decay, the irrecoverability of the past, the cost of tidiness, why heat engines need sinks, why you can't un-burn fuel or un-send a message — traces to statistics plus boundary conditions rather than to time itself having a directed physics at the fundamental level.
