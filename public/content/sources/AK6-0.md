A filter bubble is a state of intellectual isolation produced by personalized search, recommendation systems, and algorithmic curation: platforms infer what a user wants to see from their location, click history, and browsing behavior, and then serve them more of it, so that each person encounters information that reinforces their existing views and is progressively separated from content that challenges them. The term was coined by internet activist Eli Pariser around 2010 and developed in his book *The Filter Bubble: What the Internet Is Hiding from You* (2011).

Pariser argued that personalization inverted the original promise of the internet. The early web, and especially Google and social media, seemed like a universal commons where everyone saw the same information; once results and feeds became individually tailored, two people searching the same query could be handed different realities. His famous example: one user searching Google for "BP" got investment news about the oil company's stock, while another got information about the Deepwater Horizon oil spill — "strikingly different" results for identical keywords. Each user, not knowing the other's results exist, mistakes their customized window for the world.

Pariser described the mechanism in three steps: "First, you figure out who people are and what they like. Then, you provide them with content and services that best fit them. Finally, you tune in to get the fit just right. Your identity shapes your media." A *Wall Street Journal* study he cited found the top 50 internet sites installed an average of 64 tracking cookies and beacons apiece; personal data about a search for "depression" or a visit to a divorce-law page followed users around the web with targeted ads. The filters operate invisibly — users do not see the choices being made for them — and they optimize for engagement, which favors content that confirms and arouses over content that complicates.

The civic concern is that democracy depends on a shared information environment: if citizens live inside separate bubbles, they cannot argue from common facts, and exposure to opposing arguments — a precondition for deliberation — disappears. The 2016 U.S. election, the spread of fake news, and echo-chamber research brought the term into mainstream debate over whether algorithmic personalization deepens polarization.

Empirical findings have been more mixed than the strongest version of the thesis predicts: studies of actual media diets find that online exposure is often *more* diverse than in-person social networks, that many users encounter opposition incidentally, and that cable news and face-to-face sorting can be stronger echo chambers than algorithms. But the core mechanism — systems that learn from attention and serve more of what is rewarded will, absent countervailing design, narrow the field of what each person sees — remains central to debates about recommendation systems, feed governance, and media literacy, and Pariser himself later worked on "bridge rankings" and civic design to counteract it.
