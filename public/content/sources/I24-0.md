> House-curated overview of Balaji Srinivasan's The Network State (2022).
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the cited author.

*The Network State: How to Start a New Country* (Balaji Srinivasan, 2022) proposes that the next institutional innovation is neither the existing nation-state nor libertarian seasteading, but a form of polity built bottom-up from online communities: a **network state**.

His definition (from the book): a network state is a social network with moral innovation, a sense of national consciousness, an acknowledged founder and collective purpose, in-person campuses, and eventually a cryptocurrency, that can conclude treaties with existing states and secure diplomatic recognition. The recipe: start with an online community of like-minded founders around a moral cause (health, longevity, wellness, new schooling, low-density living, pro-tech values, often organized against hostile legacy institutions); build trust and revenue; hold in-person gatherings, then crowdfund apartments, neighborhoods, and finally contiguous land in many jurisdictions; use remote work (COVID having proven work could detach from location) and crypto for economic independence; grow the archipelago across countries; and eventually achieve some form of sovereignty or special-zone status.

The intellectual lineage: charter cities (Paul Romer), seasteading (Friedman & Quirk), start-up cities, cyberpunk cypherpunk exit politics, and the Sovereign Individual's bet that skilled capital and people will exit rather than reform dysfunctional states. Balaji's framing — "history is deterministic in hindsight and libertarian in foresight" — argues that exit and competition in governance serve the function market competition serves in goods: states must serve customers or lose them.

Critiques are substantial. Institutional economists note that successful states are built on centuries of legitimacy, shared identity, and collective violence capacity; gated communities and expatriate enclaves already exist and are not countries. Land-based sovereignty requires solving the problem of security, which historically means either becoming a state (violence) or paying one for protection — the network state's "many jurisdictions" structure means it never accumulates concentrated power to resist a determined territorial state. Social critics argue the exit option is anti-solidarity: the people who most need better institutions are left in the failing ones, and selective enclaves accelerate the same divergence as "elite paralysis" of public systems. Polis-building experts note new countries are rare because international law (recognition) is conservative. Critics also highlight Balaji's later trajectory — a billion-dollar Bitcoin bet and exit-oriented politics — as evidence the project is more symbolic than operational.

The durable insight regardless: governance and community attachment are partially unbundling; remote-first global communities, digital payments, and platform-native institutions are genuinely new (the post-2020 "micro-diasporas" and pop-up cities were early examples); competition between jurisdictions is increasing; and the question "what makes people consent to institutions" is live again. Network states as full countries may remain rare; network institutions (schools, medical systems, financial services, identity) that function transnationally are already arriving — which may be the book's practical future, even short of sovereignty.
