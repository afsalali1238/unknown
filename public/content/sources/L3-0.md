The **base rate fallacy** — base rate neglect — is the error of judging a case only from its particular features and ignoring the underlying prevalence (the base rate) of the relevant category. Kahneman and Tversky showed that people naturally reason by similarity and vivid specifics, not by prior frequencies: when given a personality sketch resembling a librarian, people say "librarian" even when the population has fifty times as many engineers; when given a positive test result, they trust it without asking how common the condition is.

The classic demonstration is the **false positive paradox**. Take a test that is 95% accurate (5% false positives) for a disease present in 2% of the population. In 1,000 people: 20 are sick and test positive; but 49 of the 980 healthy people also test positive. So a positive result means only 20 of 69 positives — about 29% — actually have the disease. The "highly accurate" test is wrong more often than right when applied to a rare condition, because false positives accrue across the huge negative population. The same math governs facial recognition across millions of faces, fraud flags on billions of transactions, security screening, DNA evidence in court (the "prosecutor's fallacy" confuses P(match evidence | innocent) with P(innocent | match evidence)), and spam filters. When prevalence is lower than the false-positive rate, positives are dominated by false alarms — precision is a function of base rates, not just test quality.

Bayes' theorem is the fix: P(hypothesis | evidence) ∝ P(evidence | hypothesis) × P(hypothesis). The prior — the base rate — must anchor every update. Inside view versus outside view (Kahneman's planning distinction): when forecasting a project, startup, merger, or career move, people plan from the specific case ("our team, our features"), while the outside view starts with the reference class — how long do similar projects typically take, how often do startups like this succeed, what happens to deals in this distribution. The outside view is usually better and almost always more humbling, yet psychologically harder to adopt, because our case feels special and the specifics are vivid.

Practical disciplines: (1) Ask "How common is this in general?" before reacting to a single story or signal. (2) When interpreting rare events or positive flags in low-prevalence populations, regress toward the base rate; triage results rather than acting on them directly. (3) In forecasting, construct the outside view first, then adjust for specifics. (4) Beware evidence in high-noise, low-signal environments where base rates of genuine opportunity are tiny — most fund managers, pitches, symptoms, and alarms sit in populations where priors dominate. (5) Updating should shift odds, not certainties: strong signals in thin-prior worlds still leave you uncertain.
