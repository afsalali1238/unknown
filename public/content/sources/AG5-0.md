> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the authors.

Jobs to Be Done (JTBD) is Clayton Christensen's theory of demand: customers don't buy products or services; they "hire" them to get a **job** done in their life. The theory emerged from Christensen's observation that companies knowing everything about customer demographics and product categories still fail at innovation, because those attributes don't cause purchases. A job is the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance — functional, yes, but always with emotional and social dimensions.

The founding anecdote: a fast-food chain studying milkshake sales found demographics told them nothing. Interviewing people who bought milkshakes revealed *when* and *why*: most were bought alone, early in the morning, by commuters facing a long, boring drive. They hired the milkshake to keep them full and occupied until lunch; it had to be thick enough to last the commute, clean to eat one-handed in the car, and more interesting than a banana (which was gone in two minutes) or a bagel (crumbs on the suit). Competitors for the job weren't other milkshakes — they were bananas, bagels, coffee, and the radio. Innovation meant improving the shake for *that job* (thicker, add fruit chunks for interest, move the dispenser early), not for "the milkshake customer."

The theory's key moves:

- **Circumstance, not customer attributes, defines the job.** The same person hires different products for different jobs in different contexts; segmenting by age or income misses the causal trigger for purchase.
- **Jobs are multifaceted.** Every hire has functional ("get me fed"), emotional ("make me feel I'm treating myself"), and social ("signal who I am") components. Competing on function alone loses to products that serve the whole job.
- **Innovation improves when you study the struggle, not the customer profile.** Observe people in context; identify the moment they sense a gap ("first thought"), the forces pushing them to change (push from the old, pull of the new), and the anxieties and habits holding them back (friction and inertia).
- **Non-consumption and workarounds are the richest signal.** People piecing together ugly solutions (spreadsheets, sticky notes, two apps at once) show you a job badly served — the target for disruption.

For product builders JTBD shifts the question from "What features should we add?" to "What progress is someone trying to make, and what would doing the whole job look like?" It refrains competition (any solution to the same job is a rival), marketing (sell the outcome and identity, not the spec), and metrics (retention means the product does the job; churn means you were fired). Christensen's larger claim: companies that understand the job have a more durable moat than companies chasing user traits, because jobs are stable even as products and demographics change — the milkshake job existed a century ago and will exist a century hence, while the technologies hired for it come and go.
