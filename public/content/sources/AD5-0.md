Circadian rhythms are the physical, mental, and behavioral changes an organism experiences over a roughly 24-hour cycle. Light and dark have the biggest influence on them, but food intake, stress, physical activity, social environment, and temperature also affect them. Most living things have circadian rhythms — animals, plants, and microorganisms. In humans, nearly every tissue and organ has its own circadian rhythm, and collectively they are tuned to the daily cycle of day and night. Circadian rhythms influence sleep patterns, hormone release, appetite and digestion, and body temperature.

The system that regulates an organism's innate sense of time is called the biological clock. It is composed of proteins encoded by thousands of genes that switch on and off in a specific order. A master clock coordinates all the biological clocks in an organism. In vertebrates, including humans, the master clock sits in the brain: a large group of nerve cells called the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN). Among other functions, the SCN controls production of the hormone melatonin based on the amount of light the eyes receive — in the evening the master clock tells the brain to make more melatonin, causing sleepiness — and it synchronizes the circadian rhythms in different organs and tissues across the body.

In 2017, NIGMS-funded researchers Jeffrey C. Hall, Michael Rosbash, and Michael W. Young won the Nobel Prize for their circadian rhythms work. They identified a protein in fruit flies, called PER, that controls normal daily biological rhythms. During the daytime, PER is produced by the cell but immediately broken down in the cytoplasm, keeping its levels low. When night falls, a protein called TIM binds directly to PER, protecting it from breakdown. The PER-TIM complexes enter the nucleus and stop the cell from making more PER. Then, as day breaks, the complexes break down, the block on PER transcription is lifted, and the cycle repeats. In this way, PER regulates its own synthesis through a negative feedback loop — the same engineering principle as a thermostat, which turns the furnace off when the set temperature is reached and back on when it falls below.

Circadian rhythms can fall out of sync with the outside world: gene variants can affect the proteins that control biological clocks; neurological diseases such as Alzheimer's can disrupt rhythms and cause day-night symptom changes; jet lag and shift work alter the sleep-wake cycle; and light from electronic devices at night can confuse the clocks. Short-term desynchronization causes drowsiness, poor coordination, and difficulty with learning and focus. Long-term sleep loss and continually shifting rhythms increase the risks of obesity, diabetes, mood disorders, heart and blood-pressure problems, and cancer, and can worsen existing illness.

The biomedical lesson is that the body is not one clock but a federation of thousands of peripheral clocks held in alignment by the SCN and the light-dark cycle. When the master conductor is ignored — by night shifts, late screens, or irregular meal times — the peripheral clocks drift out of phase with one another, and metabolism, immunity, and cognition pay the price.
