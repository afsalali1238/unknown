The uncertainty principle, introduced by Werner Heisenberg in 1927, is a fundamental result of quantum mechanics: there is an irreducible limit to the precision with which certain pairs of physical properties — position and momentum above all — can be known simultaneously. The more precisely position is pinned down, the less precisely momentum can be pinned down, and vice versa. The formal inequality, derived by Earle Kennard in 1927, is σx·σp ≥ ℏ/2, where ℏ is the reduced Planck constant.

The most important correction to the popular version: the uncertainty is not a limitation of clumsy instruments disturbing a particle that secretly has definite properties. It is a property of wave-like systems. In wave mechanics, a particle's state is a wave packet. A sharply localized wave packet (precise position) must be built by superposing waves of many different wavelengths (hence momenta); a single definite wavelength (precise momentum) is spread uniformly through space. A function and its Fourier transform cannot both be sharply localized — the same tradeoff appears in classical waves, in sound (a brief click contains a broad spread of frequencies), and in signal processing (time–frequency bandwidth). Quantum mechanics simply makes this mathematical fact physical, because in quantum theory the wave packet is the complete description of the particle: the sharp values don't exist to be measured, rather than merely hidden from us.

Heisenberg offered a vivid (and partly misleading) gamma-ray-microscope heuristic: measuring an electron's position with a very short-wavelength photon kicks it unpredictably. Bohr insisted the deeper lesson was wave–particle duality — systems exhibit definite position *or* definite momentum depending on the experimental arrangement, and the two "complementary" descriptions cannot both be fully expressed at once. The principle generalizes to other conjugate pairs — energy/time, angular angle/angular momentum — and is baked into the mathematical structure of non-commuting observables (qp − pq = iℏ), which Heisenberg wrote down in 1925.

Conceptually the principle ended the classical picture of a fully deterministic, clockwork universe at the micro level: a state does not consist of exact positions and momenta waiting to be read. In practice it sets the scale of atomic stability (electrons cannot fall into nuclei), governs natural line widths of spectra, and underlies quantum fluctuations, virtual particles, and the zero-point energy of empty space. At human scales the ℏ limit is far below any measurement noise, which is why the macroscopic world appears classical and deterministic — a boundary condition that echoes the lessons of other precision limits in this collection: some uncertainty is intrinsic to the structure of the system, not to the quality of our tools.
