> House-curated summary of David Einhorn's May 2008 Ira Sohn presentation on Lehman Brothers.
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the cited speakers.

On May 21, 2008, David Einhorn of Greenlight Capital presented at the Ira Sohn Investment Research Conference a short case on Lehman Brothers, four months before its September 15, 2008 bankruptcy filing — then the largest in U.S. history. The speech, "Private Profits and Socialized Risk," argued Lehman was hiding losses through accounting choices, principally **Repo 105**: a form of balance-sheet window-dressing where Lehman sold assets (typically at quarter-end) under repurchase agreements that it classified as true sales rather than financing transactions. Under GAAP rules requiring 105%+ collateral, the transactions let Lehman remove ~$50 billion of assets and the associated debt from its reported quarter-end balance sheet, making leverage look lower — then unwind days later and return the assets. It was, as Anton Valukas's later bankruptcy examiner report concluded, a "materially misleading" accounting gambit the firm's own docs referred to internally as window dressing.

Einhorn's analytical method — now taught as forensic shorting: (1) **Follow the disclosures that don't reconcile.** Lehman reported $4.2 billion in gains on "excess" bond hedges in a quarter when the index it hedged with had moved against it; Einhorn pored over footnotes and earnings call responses, catching CFO Erin Callan contradicting the numbers — the "$1.1 billion discrepancy" the acquirersmultiple piece documents. (2) **Attack the valuation assumptions.** Lehman marked its ~$40 billion commercial real-estate and leveraged-loan book far above where secondary-market transactions were pricing, attributing the gap to illiquidity rather than losses. (3) **Incentives and culture.** Management's public dismissiveness ("I think we're confident") combined with issuance of new equity that was being diluted suggested they knew more than they said.

The Lehman board and regulators did not act; the stock rallied initially against Einhorn's position (short attacks are usually greeted by management calling the analyst ignorant and markets demanding the shorter's blood), which is the classic squeeze phase of a fundamental short. The timeline rewarded the analysis: after Fannie/Freddie went into conservatorship and Merrill sold itself to BofA, Lehman failed to find a buyer; the Fed and Treasury, burned by Bear Stearns criticism and having no legal authority to capital-fill a holding company, let Lehman file. The global financial crisis accelerated within hours.

Lessons carried since: audited numbers are management's estimates dressed as precision; in a deleveraging cycle, firms with mismarked assets and repo dependence face death spirals as collateral calls and ratings triggers compound; the "who's next?" analysis after Bear Stearns (short-sellers systematically went through leveraged balance sheets — Lehman, then AIG, then the monolines) was correct sequencing; and forensic shorts do public service by exposing accounting games early, even though they're attacked for it — Einhorn's presentation is a primary source for "trust the footnotes, not the headlines." Repo 105 also permanently changed how analysts treat quarter-end leverage: reporting dates are now understood to be managed moments.
