> House-curated overview of axiomatic belief systems.
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the cited authors.

Belief systems are not flat lists of claims; they are architectures, and the load-bearing elements are axioms — premises that are not derived from anything else within the system but determine what counts as evidence and argument below them. Aristotle put the structural fact precisely: not everything can be demonstrated; anyone who demands a proof of every premise destroys proof itself, because every proof must start from something not proven ( Posterior Analytics). A chain of justification either regresses forever, loops, or terminates at axioms held without derivation — Agrippa's trilemma. Foundationalism is the conclusion that all belief systems rest on ground-floor commitments.

Axioms come in several kinds:

- **Formal axioms:** the axioms of logic and mathematics (identity, non-contradiction, induction assumptions). You cannot prove logic without logic; Peano axioms can't be derived from arithmetic.
- **Epistemic axioms:** trust in the senses, in the uniformity of nature (required for induction), in other people's testimony, in the external world's existence, in the general reliability of your own reason.
- **Moral axioms:** bedrock value commitments — that suffering matters, that fairness is a demand, or conversely that loyalty/order dominate. Moral arguments can derive consequences from shared values but cannot produce a value from pure facts (Hume: no ought from is).
- **Identity axioms:** the sacred or central commitments of a self or group — nation, religion, freedom, solidarity — which people hold the way they hold membership rather than the way they hold hypotheses.

The architecture explains why argument over controversial surface questions often fails: disputants are proving theorems from different axioms, and each side's evidence is only evidence given its ground layer. You cannot rationally convince a person out of a commitment their reason itself stands on using tools granted by that commitment. Kuhn's paradigms, Wittgenstein's "hinge propositions" ("the questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn"), and Quine's web of belief (beliefs meet experience as a corporate body, with core beliefs more insulated than periphery) describe the same structure.

Implications: (1) Inspect your own axioms — most are inherited (culture, family, era) rather than chosen, and mistaking an axiom for a conclusion is the dominant form of self-deception. (2) In persuasion, argue at the highest shared layer; when surface facts fail, the disagreement is axiomatic — surface debate is then theater, and the real work is hinge-level: trust, lived experience, narrative. (3) Defend the epistemic axioms of rational discourse (honesty, evidence, good faith) because once they're abandoned, nothing downstream is enforceable. (4) Expect pluralism: axiom systems are underdetermined by evidence (many worldviews fit the same data), which is why civil peace needs tolerance between irreducible foundations. (5) Watch for axioms masquerading as facts in yourself — the beliefs you would treat as madness to question are the ones shaping everything else.
