In economics, a perverse incentive is an incentive structure with undesirable results — particularly one where those effects are unexpected and contrary to the intentions of its designers. The worst case is the **cobra effect**, where people are incentivized to make the very problem worse. The term was coined by economist Horst Siebert in 2001, based on an anecdote from the British Raj.

According to the story, the British government in Delhi, concerned about venomous cobras, offered a bounty for every dead cobra. Initially the policy worked — large numbers of snakes were killed. But eventually people began breeding cobras for the income. When the government noticed and scrapped the reward, the cobra breeders set their now-worthless snakes free. The wild cobra population ended up *higher* than before the program began.

The same pattern recurs wherever a measurable proxy is rewarded instead of the outcome itself. In 1902, the French colonial government in Hanoi, facing a rat infestation, paid a bounty per rat tail. Rat catchers soon began capturing rats, severing their tails, and releasing the tailless rats back into the sewers so they could breed and produce more bounty. A U.S. Army post in Georgia paid $40 per feral-pig tail; hunters targeted large trophy males (whose removal barely affects population growth) while the bait-feed used to attract pigs improved the animals' nutrition and raised fertility and offspring survival — the pig population increased under the program.

Other modern examples: a 2002 program paying Afghan farmers to destroy poppy crops ignited a planting frenzy as farmers rushed to grow poppies eligible for payouts; 2021 U.S. rules meant to prevent sesame cross-contamination led many food companies to simply add sesame as a labeled ingredient, increasing the number of sesame-containing products; and gun buyback programs at flat rates prompted people to 3D-print crude firearms solely to turn them in for cash.

The general mechanism: incentives measure inputs or countable artifacts, but the agents being incentivized optimize for the measure, not for the goal. This is closely related to Goodhart's Law ("when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure") and to Campbell's law. The lesson for system designers is that an incentive does not just reward behavior — it *teaches the system where to game*, and the more reward is attached to a proxy, the more effort flows into producing the proxy without producing what it was supposed to stand for.
