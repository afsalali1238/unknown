The search for life beyond Earth is conducted almost entirely through atmospheric forensics. We cannot visit exoplanets; we analyze starlight filtered through their atmospheres. When a planet transits its star, a fraction of the starlight passes through the planet's atmosphere; molecules absorb specific wavelengths, leaving spectral fingerprints. Non-transiting worlds can be studied via thermal emission and reflected light. The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), launched in 2021, is the first instrument with enough sensitivity to do this for rocky planets — notably the seven Earth-sized planets of TRAPPIST-1, a red dwarf 40 light-years away, three to four of which orbit in the habitable zone where liquid water could exist.

**Biosignatures** are chemicals or disequilibrium patterns best explained by life. The leading candidate is oxygen (or its proxy ozone) plus a reducing gas like methane: oxygen and methane react and destroy each other, so finding both coexisting means something continuously replenishes them — on Earth, life. Oxygen alone is not decisive: abiotic water photolysis can produce it. Other candidates: nitrous oxide (largely biological here), dimethyl sulfide (on Earth emitted almost exclusively by phytoplankton), seasonal cycles in gas concentrations, and the red-edge reflectance of vegetation (chlorophyll's sharp infrared reflectivity jump). Every proposed biosignature faces the false-positive problem: astrophysics, geology, and ocean chemistry can mimic them. The history of Mars canals and the disputed 1996 ALH84001 meteorite is the field's cautionary canon — extraordinary claims require ruling out every abiotic mechanism.

**Technosignatures** are fingerprints of technology: industrial gases in an atmosphere (CFCs — no known natural source; nitrogen dioxide from combustion); artificial illumination or thermal signatures; large structures (Dyson-swarm-like dimming, megastructures' waste heat — the SETI program's revived scope); narrowband radio signals; even planetary-scale chemistry that only industry produces (technofossils of an energy-intensive civilization). The framework broadens the search from "are they alive?" to "do they have an industrial phase at all?" — and connects to the Fermi question.

Red dwarfs like TRAPPIST-1 complicate the picture: they are flare-active, bathing planets in UV and X-rays that can strip atmospheres (JWST measurements of TRAPPIST-1 b and c found no thick atmospheres), while synchronous rotation and flare chemistry can generate abiotic false positives. Results so far have been cautionary: JWST's early atmospheric spectra have thinned rather than thickened hopes for the most-studied worlds.

The deeper pattern is signal detection under extreme uncertainty: with one data point (Earth), priors dominate; every "sign of life" must be weighed against a growing catalog of abiotic mimics; and the field self-polices by designing observations to falsify abiotic explanations rather than confirm life. It is empirical Bayes under cosmic distance — the same discipline that governs rare-event reasoning anywhere: absence of evidence accumulates quietly (no signals across billions of channels, no biosignatures in the first well-measured worlds), and a positive claim must carry the burden of ruling out the ordinary.
