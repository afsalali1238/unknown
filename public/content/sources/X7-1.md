The panopticon is a design of institutional building with an inbuilt system of control, originated by the English philosopher and social reformer Jeremy Bentham in the late eighteenth century. The concept is to allow all prisoners of an institution to be observed by a single guard, without the inmates ever knowing whether they are being watched.

It is physically impossible for one guard to watch every cell at once. That does not matter: because the inmates cannot know when they are watched, they are motivated to act as though they are watched at all times. They become, in effect, their own guards. The architecture is a rotunda with an inspection house at its center, backlit so that the watcher is a silhouette; cells ring the circumference, each visible from the center through a system of blinds and lighting. Bentham conceived the plan as equally applicable to prisons, factories, hospitals, schools, and asylums — anywhere supervision could be made constant in its *effects* without being constant in fact.

The word derives from the Greek *panoptes*, "all seeing." Bentham sketched the idea in letters written while visiting his brother Samuel in Russia in 1786–87, where Samuel had applied constant-observation principles to a factory workforce; Bentham transferred the idea from workers to prisoners and commissioned architectural drawings from Willey Reveley. Notably, Bentham also applied the inspection principle upward: the gaoler himself was to be observed by public officials and visitors, answering the ancient question *quis custodiet ipsos custodes?* — who guards the guards? Bentham spent decades trying to build the panopticon and remained bitter that the plan was never adopted in his lifetime.

The panopticon became enormously influential after the French philosopher Michel Foucault used it in *Discipline and Punish* (1975) as the central metaphor for modern power. Foucault argued that the panopticon is not just a prison design but a diagram of how discipline works in modern society: power is most efficient when it is visible and unverifiable — you *see* the tower, but you never know whether you are currently watched. The observed internalize the gaze and police themselves, so that actual coercion becomes rare; surveillance becomes the self-administered discipline of schools, workplaces, and eventually digital platforms.

The metaphor was revived with fresh force in debates over workplace surveillance, monitoring software, and state data collection. Bentham thought the panopticon humane and economical — replacing punishment with the mere possibility of observation. Foucault saw something darker: the perfection of a power that is "all-seeing" precisely because it no longer needs to see.
