"The Strength of Weak Ties" is a 1973 paper by sociologist Mark Granovetter, published in the *American Journal of Sociology*, and one of the most cited papers in the social sciences. Its counterintuitive finding: the relationships that matter most for getting information, finding jobs, and spreading ideas are not the close, strong friendships — they are the loose, distant connections.

Granovetter surveyed professional workers who had recently changed jobs and asked how they found their new positions through personal contacts. Many reported that the contact who told them about the job was not a close friend but an acquaintance — someone they saw rarely, a former colleague, a friend of a friend. Strong ties are clustered: your close friends know roughly what you know, move in the same circles, and hear about the same openings. Weak ties bridge different clusters: an acquaintance lives in a different social world, hears different rumors, knows different opportunities. The weak tie is a bridge between otherwise disconnected parts of the social graph.

This leads to the paper's famous paradox: strong ties are valuable for support and trust, but weak ties are valuable for *novelty*. Information flows densely inside a group but sparsely between groups; the person who connects groups has access to everything both groups know. Granovetter quantified tie strength as a combination of time spent together, emotional intensity, intimacy, and reciprocity — and showed that the more of these a relationship has, the more the two people's other relationships overlap, until a strong-tie network becomes a closed echo chamber.

The community-level consequence is just as striking. Granovetter later argued that neighborhoods with many weak ties — people who know each other casually but not deeply — often organize collectively better than neighborhoods whose tight cliques fragment and distrust outsiders. The loose fabric of acquaintances makes rumors, recruitment, and collective action spread; the absence of bridging ties leaves isolated pockets unable to coordinate.

The paper became foundational for network science, viral marketing, recruiting, and the study of social media. It predicts the success of platforms built on "followers" rather than friends (loose ties transmit, strong ties bind), explains why diverse weak-tie networks outperform comfortable cliques for idea flow, and reframes a common intuition: staying in touch with acquaintances is not shallow networking — it is how the social graph gets bridges that strong ties can never build.
