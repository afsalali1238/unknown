The Matthew effect — also called the Matthew principle or cumulative advantage — is the tendency for success to accrue disproportionately to those who already have it: the rich get richer, the famous get more credit, the well-connected accumulate more connections. It takes its name from the Gospel of Matthew's Parable of the Talents: "For to every one who has will more be given, and he will have abundance; but from him who has not, even what he has will be taken away."

Sociologists Robert K. Merton and Harriet Zuckerman coined the term in 1968 while studying how scientists receive recognition. They found that eminent scientists get disproportionately more credit for the same contributions than unknown researchers: a prize will almost always go to the most senior member of a project even if a graduate student did the work; a paper from a famous lab is cited more than the identical paper from an obscure one. Stephen Stigler later half-jokingly codified this as Stigler's law of eponymy: "No scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer" — itself credited to Stigler's father.

The same pattern turned up elsewhere. In the sociology of education, children who start as slightly better readers read more, get more attention and more rewarding materials, and pull further ahead; children who start slightly behind read less, fall further behind, and often stop — early gaps compound over years. In network science, Derek de Solla Price described in 1976 how new papers and links preferentially attach to already well-connected nodes: **preferential attachment** — "the rich get richer" as a growth rule — produces scale-free networks, where a few hubs have vastly more connections than the average node. Albert-László Barabási and Réka Albert's 1999 model showed this rule alone explains the power-law structure of the web, citation graphs, and many other networks.

Economically, cumulative advantage means that small early differences in resources compound: those with capital can take risks that recover from losses, wait out downturns, and buy assets during forced sales; those without capital face ruin from the same shocks. Merit and luck interact — the effect does not say success is undeserved, but it says success is strongly *path-dependent*, so the distribution of rewards overstates the distribution of underlying talent.

For Merton the normative point mattered: the Matthew effect is a distortion of the reward system science relies on. If credit flows to the already-credited, the scientific community misallocates its attention — readers follow the famous names and miss the unknown discoverers. The same insight generalizes to reputation, funding, attention, and platform power: advantage in these systems is self-reinforcing, which means early advantages matter more than they appear to, and apparent meritocracies tend to harden into oligarchies unless the feedback loop is deliberately countered.
