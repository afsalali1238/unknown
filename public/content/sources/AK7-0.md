Agenda-setting theory holds that the news media, by choosing which issues to publicize and how prominently, determine what the public considers important — not what to think, but what to think *about*. The theory was formally developed by Maxwell McCombs and Donald Shaw in their 1972 paper "The Agenda-Setting Function of Mass Media," based on the 1968 U.S. presidential election.

In the "Chapel Hill study," McCombs and Shaw asked 100 undecided voters what they considered the most important issues of the campaign, then coded what local news media had covered most. They found a near-perfect correspondence: the issues ranked highest in the news were ranked highest by voters. The media did not tell people how to vote on Vietnam or civil rights, but they set the shortlist of problems voters believed the election was about. The result matched Walter Lippmann's much earlier observation, in *Public Opinion* (1922), that citizens respond not to the real environment but to a "pseudo-environment" constructed by the press — "the world outside and the pictures in our heads."

The mechanism is cognitive: agenda setting works through **accessibility**. The frequency and prominence of news coverage makes certain issues easier to recall; when people are asked what the country's most important problem is, they answer with the issue most available in memory — typically the one news media emphasized most. The effect comes not from one message but from the cumulative weight of many different stories all touching the same general issue. It is reinforced by **gatekeeping**: editors and producers decide which events are newsworthy, which perspectives are included, and which stories never become public.

The theory developed three levels. **First-level** agenda setting: the media tell us which *issues* matter. **Second-level** (attribute) agenda setting: by emphasizing certain attributes — which aspects of a candidate or event are covered — the media shape how we *frame* the issue, not just whether we notice it. **Third-level** agenda setting: the media also shape which issues and attributes the public mentally associates with one another, network-style.

The implication, noted by Bernard Cohen and made famous by the theory, is that "the press may not be successful much of the time in telling people what to think, but it is stunningly successful in telling its readers what to think about." For a society this means political power runs partly through attention: control the agenda of what is discussable and visible, and you control the policy options that can even be considered, without ever issuing an order. The theory became one of the most studied effects in mass communication, and maps directly onto later debates about algorithmic feeds, trending topics, and platform moderation: whoever sets the visible agenda sets the boundaries of public reason.
