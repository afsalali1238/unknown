> House-curated overview of William MacAskill's What We Owe the Future (2022) and reviews.
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the cited authors.

*What We Owe the Future* is philosopher William MacAskill's argument for **longtermism**: moral action should prioritize the interests of future people because they will vastly outnumber the living and are completely powerless to affect our choices, while our choices today shape their existence.

The numbers anchor the case. Human civilization over the next few centuries could run to the end of a habitable Earth (~500 million years) or, if spread beyond it, to cosmological scales; even on conservative estimates, future persons outnumber the current population by orders of magnitude (e.g., 10^12 future people vs. 10^10 living). Expected-value reasoning then gives enormous weight to shifting the probability of stable, flourishing civilizations by even small amounts. MacAskill identifies three priorities that do this:

1. **Averting existential risks** — pandemics (engineered), nuclear war, runaway AI, extreme climate states. These are risks that, if realized, truncate or permanently damage the entire future, destroying trillions of lives in expectation.
2. **Moral change and values** — moral values have shifted dramatically (slavery abolition, women's rights); future people's values will shape the long civilizations; MacAskill argues moral progress itself is a cause to support, citing near-misses of locked-in moral horror (he notes the Confederacy coming close to survival in alternate histories would have changed the moral trajectory of centuries).
3. **Ensuring good conditions for the long future** — building institutions, norms, and capabilities now; population and civilization can either reach stable wealth or stumble into permanently bad equilibria.

The book's distinctive move over earlier effective-altruist writing is accessibility and breadth: longtermism is presented not as a niche utility-maximizing doctrine but as the natural extension of standard moral concern — people care about grandchildren; the logic extends. It uses vivid cases: the early abolitionists who invested lifetimes in a cause with low apparent chance (an argument about persistence), the moral disasters of near-history, and value-fragility.

Critiques include: the future persons puzzle (non-identity problem: *which* future people exist depends on what we do, so "harm to them" has odd structure); risk of fanaticism — tiny probabilities of astronomically large futures can dominate practical reasoning, which critics call a reductio; empirical critiques that future people will be vastly richer and better equipped, so altruism toward them resembles sacrificing the poor of today for the wealthy of tomorrow (though existential risk is symmetric — it also includes future poor); political critiques that longtermism depoliticizes present injustice and can be used to justify concentrating resources in wealthy institutions; and the "empirical model uncertainty" problem — predictions about civilization 1,000 years out are highly uncalibrated. The book's defense: most of the recommended actions (pandemic preparedness, AI safety, anti-lockdown-of-values norms, reducing nuclear risk) are also good present actions, and the long frame strengthens rather than replaces near-term moral claims. The lasting contribution is making "future lives count in our decisions" a mainstream moral concept.
