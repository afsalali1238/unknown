A threshold model is one where behavior changes qualitatively once a variable crosses a critical value. In the study of collective behavior, Mark Granovetter — building on Thomas Schelling — proposed in his 1978 paper "Threshold Models of Collective Behavior" that individuals decide whether to join a collective action (a riot, a strike, an adoption, leaving a party, endorsing an opinion) based on how many others have already done so. A person's **threshold** is "the number or proportion of others who must make one decision before a given actor does so."

People have different thresholds, determined by disposition, economics, social ties, and information: radicals with threshold 0 join immediately; cautious people at threshold 80 only join once nearly everyone has; everyone in between cascades in sequence once their threshold is met. Granovetter's striking result: the *aggregate outcome depends almost entirely on the distribution of thresholds*, and tiny changes in that distribution can swing the system between total quiescence and total mobilization. His canonical example: two situations that are "nearly identical" in individual preferences produce either a full riot or no riot at all, because a chain reaction either completes or stalls. Knowing average preferences tells you almost nothing; the ordering and threshold distribution tells you everything.

Schelling had shown the same logic earlier (1971) in his segregation model and his treatment of "tipping": a neighborhood can tip from mixed to segregated, or an audience from one choice to another, even when no one strongly prefers the end state — each individual only requires a small trigger, and local interactions cascade. "There is no simple correspondence of individual incentive to collective results."

The framework explains fads, technology adoption, bank runs, protest mobilization, livestream chat dynamics, and product/format tipping on platforms. Practical corollaries: (1) **seeding matters** — the first visible movers supply the threshold for everyone else, so early signals (visible customers, usage counters, endorsements) are disproportionately powerful; (2) **small interventions near the tipping threshold can outperform large ones far from it**; (3) the same person with the same preferences can join or abstain depending only on what they believe others will do, making expectations self-fulfilling; (4) predicting cascades requires mapping the threshold distribution and network structure — not averaging attitudes. Threshold models also underlie the spiral of silence, information cascades, and modern cascade-based views of marketing and political action.
