The question "What was there before the Big Bang?" may be ill-posed in the same way "What is north of the North Pole?" is — but modern physics is increasingly exploring a stranger possibility: that spacetime itself is not fundamental, but an **emergent** phenomenon — a high-level description that arises from something underneath, the way temperature emerges from molecular motion or liquidity from water molecules.

The roots are established physics. We already know gravity and thermodynamics are linked: black holes carry entropy proportional to their horizon area (Bekenstein–Hawking), and Hawking showed they radiate at a temperature set by surface gravity. Jacob Bekenstein's formula treats spacetime geometry as something that can be counted in states. The holographic principle ('t Hooft, Susskind) then says the information inside any region of space may be fully encoded on its boundary — a strong hint that volume spacetime is a projection of boundary data. The AdS/CFT correspondence (Maldacena, 1997) made this precise: a gravitational theory in a curved "bulk" spacetime is mathematically equivalent to a quantum field theory without gravity on the boundary — gravity appearing as a higher-level description of ordinary quantum physics one level down.

**Entropic/emergent gravity** programs (Erik Verlinde's "emergent gravity" 2010/2017; earlier Sakharov's 1967 induced gravity) turn this into a derivation: gravity is not a fundamental force but an entropic effect arising from the statistical behavior of information encoded on surfaces; in Verlinde's version, dark matter phenomena (flat galaxy rotation curves) can be approximated without dark matter, as an emergent effect of the displacement of entropy in de Sitter space — controversial and empirically contested, but representative of a research wave treating spacetime as a thermodynamic condensate. Quantum foundations add independent approaches: loop quantum gravity quantizes space itself into granular "spins" (space emerging from combinatorial structure); causal-set theory builds spacetime from discrete causal events; quantum information approaches (tensor networks, it-from-qubit) derive geometry from entanglement structure — "entanglement builds space" (Raamsdonk): regions of spacetime appear connected because their underlying degrees of freedom are entangled, and destroying the entanglement dissolves the connection.

Cosmological implications unsettle the "before" question. The Big Bang is where our well-tested theories break: general relativity produces a singularity, meaning its own limits. Candidates: eternal inflation (our Big Bang is one bubble in an infinitely inflating multiverse; "before" is other bubbles); the Hartle–Hawking no-boundary proposal (time as we know it doesn't extend back — near the origin, time behaves like a spatial direction, so there is no t=0 edge); conformal cyclic cosmology (Penrose; universe cycles through aeons); bounce models (a contracting phase preceded ours). Brian Greene's popular accounts emphasize that "before the Big Bang" presupposes time, which may itself be an emergent property of the post-Big Bang universe.

The recurring conceptual lesson — the same one as emergence elsewhere — is that fundamental questions can be category errors. Asking what spacetime is made of may be like asking what a temperature is made of: the answer appears at a lower level, and the lower level need not possess properties of the higher (no "hot" molecules; no space in the theory of space). Whether any of these programs is right remains open; what they've established is that treating spacetime as derivative, not primitive, is now mainstream research rather than speculation.
