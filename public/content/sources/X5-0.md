Quantum suicide is a thought experiment, descended from Schrödinger's cat, that examines the many-worlds interpretation (MWI) of quantum mechanics from the *inside*. Setup: a device kills the experimenter with 50% probability depending on a quantum measurement — each run branches the universe into a live branch and a dead branch. Under a collapse interpretation (Copenhagen), after enough runs the experimenter is almost certainly dead. Under MWI, every run produces both branches; in some branches a version of the experimenter survives every iteration. Since — barring an afterlife — only the surviving versions can have conscious experience, the experimenter's subjective path always continues along the surviving branches. **Quantum immortality** names the conjectured extension: that subjectively one can never experience death, because one's consciousness always follows the branch where one lives.

The idea was discussed by Euan Squires (1986), Hans Moravec (1987), Bruno Marchal, and Max Tegmark (1998); Hugh Everett privately believed his many-worlds theory guaranteed him some kind of immortality, though biographers note it only guarantees that the majority of your copies die — "hardly a rational goal."

Physicists and philosophers almost uniformly reject it as a practical guide to anything. The objections are serious and cumulative:

- It assumes MWI is true, plus a controversial personal-identity metaphysics — that future selves in separate branches are "you" in the relevant sense, so that surviving branches constitute your survival.
- It assumes death is a binary, instantaneous quantum event. Real death is a gradual, non-superposed process; there is usually a continuum of severely injured, slowly dying branches. The branch where you walk away unscathed may have vanishingly tiny weight, and probability (measure) across branches is what MWI says matters for expectation.
- Even MWI assigns branch weights (the squared amplitude). Repeated near-certain death leaves survival branches of astronomically tiny measure — "you" overwhelmingly end in branches of agony or nonexistence, and there is no agreed sense in which consciousness "tracks" high-measure branches.

As Anthony Aguirre warns, "it would be foolish (and selfish) in the extreme to let this possibility guide one's actions in any life-and-death question." The lasting value of the thought experiment is not its conclusion but the questions it exposes: how probability works in branching universes, what survival consists in when multiple continuants exist, and the difference between third-person and first-person evidence. It is a rigorous-feeling intuition pump whose premises — identity, measure, and the nature of death — are precisely the unproven parts, a textbook case of a logically consistent edifice built on assumptions that do the actual work.
