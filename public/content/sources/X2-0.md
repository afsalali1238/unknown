> House-curated overview of metacognition research — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metacognition
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the cited authors.

Metacognition is thinking about thinking — the mind's capacity to monitor, evaluate, and regulate its own processing. Developmental psychologists (Flavell, 1976, coined the term) and modern cognitive science treat it as a hierarchy of levels.

Level 0 — **raw cognition:** perceiving, associating, reacting; the automatic processes shared with other animals. Level 1 — **object-level reasoning:** solving the problem in front of you, using models and information. Level 2 — **metacognitive monitoring:** noticing your own confidence, confusion, and knowledge gaps ("I don't actually understand this"); knowing whether you know (calibration). Level 3 — **metacognitive control / strategy selection:** choosing *how* to think — when to slow down, when to trust intuition, which model to deploy, when to abandon an approach. Level 4 — **meta-metacognition (reflective wisdom):** noticing the patterns in how your mind goes wrong across time — your characteristic biases, motivated reasoning, emotional triggers — and designing systems that route around them (checklists, environments, commitments, advisers).

The research results are practical and slightly humbling. Competence and self-monitoring are correlated — the Dunning–Kruger effect: low performers lack the skill needed to detect low performance, so ignorance is accompanied by confidence. Calibration studies show confidence and accuracy are only weakly aligned; experts in predictably noisy domains (forecasting, punditry) are often worse calibrated than simple algorithms. Effective learners distinguish *fluency* (the text feels familiar) from *mastery* (you can reconstruct it with the book closed) — re-reading produces fluency illusions; self-testing (active recall, Feynman technique) forces metacognitive confrontation. People who plan with the outside view, pre-mortems, and base rates are essentially forcing metacognition: interrupting the confident inside-view story with perspective on their own reasoning.

Robert Kegan's adult-development framework adds a social layer: minds evolve from "socialized" (our thoughts are authored by our tribe's expectations; we don't see that we have a worldview) to "self-authoring" (we can examine our beliefs, choose commitments) to "self-transforming" (we can hold our own frameworks as objects to question and revise). Most organizations and media operate at socialized level — feeding identity rather than reflection.

Training the hierarchy: write down predictions and check them (calibration journals); use explicit checklists where error is costly; name your emotions before deciding; argue the opposite case better than your opponent; expose yourself to people whose incentives differ from yours; study cognitive biases as a taxonomy of your own failure modes; and treat the quality of your inner monologue's questions — "what would I advise a friend?" "what would make me wrong?" "what would I believe if I had no identity in this?" — as the main lever, because metacognition is the only skill whose improvement upgrades every other skill at once.
