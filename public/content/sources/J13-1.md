James Carse's *Finite and Infinite Games* distinguishes two ways of playing. **Finite games** are played to win: they have fixed rules, known boundaries, a clear end, and winners and losers. Players parade status and past trophies; they live partly in the past, where their winnings are. They need **power** to beat opponents in each contest, and they **train** — "to be prepared against surprise is to be trained."

**Infinite games** are played to keep the game going: no terminal victory, shifting rules, the goal being continued play for its own sake. Infinite players look to the future and to what's possible, don't maintain past status, and need **endurance and strength** rather than power over others. They **educate** — "to be prepared for surprise is to be educated." Finite play is serious; infinite play is joyous.

Simon Sinek later applied the frame to business: companies playing finite chase quarterly wins and rivals; companies playing infinite build capacity to outlast, choosing rivals and metrics that sustain the broader game (e.g., an industry's health). Mismatches are dangerous — an infinite player against a finite one cannot be beaten because the finite player's "win" doesn't end the game; a person playing infinite goals (relationships, craft, health) inside finite contests (rankings, deals) measures wrong things and burns out. The diagnostic question: which game are you actually in, and are your habits — training vs. education, power vs. strength, past trophies vs. future positioning — matched to it?
