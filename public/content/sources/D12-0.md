> Sequoia Capital presentation, May 2022, updating its RIP Good Times framework for the post-ZIRP tightening cycle.
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with Sequoia Capital.

"Adapting to Endure" (Sequoia, May 2022) updated the firm's "RIP Good Times" playbook for a new kind of correction: not a sudden financial collapse like 2008 but a regime change after a decade of zero-interest-rate policy (ZIRP). With inflation forcing rate hikes, money was becoming expensive again after years of being free, and the markets had begun repricing risk while most founders were still operating by 2021 rules.

The thesis: the 2020–2021 era of "growth at any price" was over, not pausing. Cheap capital had funded an abnormal environment where companies raised years of runway, hired ahead of revenue, and sold equity at prices with no relationship to sustainable economics. When discount rates rise, the math reverses: the value of distant future cash flows shrinks, investors demand real efficiency, and companies dependent on repeated fundraising face a market that no longer pays for them. Crucially, the deck framed this not as a short bear market to wait out but as a multi-year transition requiring fundamental adaptation — the title says endure, not survive-and-restart.

The prescriptions mirrored but updated 2008:

- **Assume the recovery rules have changed.** Don't plan for a return to 2021 multiples; base forecasts on tighter conditions for an extended period.
- **Get to default-alive.** Cut burn to achieve profitability or at least demonstrable, credible path to self-sustaining cash flows; extend runway to 2–3 years or to profitability.
- **Cut decisively, not serially.** Large, earlier layoffs with compassion outperform waves of small cuts that keep organizations in fear.
- **Re-justify every dollar of spending against return on investment**; freeze experimental headcount, tighten hiring standards, focus teams on the core product and customers who value it most.
- **Protect the core and use the downturn offensively where possible.** Discipline is survival, but Sequoia emphasized that great companies are *forged* in downturns — competitors retreat, talent becomes available, and companies that keep shipping take durable share (the deck pointed to companies that emerged from 2008–2009 stronger).

The cultural and psychological advice mattered as much as the financial: founders should communicate honestly with employees and investors, lead with clarity under uncertainty, and avoid the two failure modes — panic-driven cuts that destroy capability and denial-driven spending that destroys runway. The memo's broader significance was signaling the venture industry's official turn from growth-at-any-cost (the era of the 2021 mega-rounds) to efficiency culture, which translated industry-wide into layoffs, valuation write-downs, and the "default alive" vocabulary. Its implicit critique applied to the whole tech ecosystem: businesses that only function with free money are interest-rate artifacts, not companies.
