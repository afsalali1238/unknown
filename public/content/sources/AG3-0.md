*The Design of Everyday Things* is a best-selling book by cognitive scientist and usability engineer Donald Norman, first published in 1988 under the title *The Psychology of Everyday Things*. A revised and expanded edition appeared in 2013.

The book's premise is that design is the communication between object and user, and that this conduit should be optimized to make using the object pleasurable. Norman's central argument is that when people fumble with everyday objects — doors that push when they should pull, stoves whose knobs don't map to their burners, projector controls that defeat a roomful of conference speakers — it is not the user's fault. People blame themselves, but the real failure is the design's lack of intuitive guidance.

Norman introduced and popularized several terms that became foundational. An **affordance** is a possible interaction between an object and its user: a door affords pushing or pulling, a knob affords turning. (He borrowed the concept from psychologist James J. Gibson.) In the 2013 edition he added **signifiers**: the signals that communicate where the action happens — a flat plate on a door signals "push," a rounded bar signals "pull." Good design makes the correct action discoverable without labels.

He also popularized **user-centered design**: design built from the needs and mental models of the user rather than from engineering convenience or aesthetics alone. Its principles include simplifying the structure of tasks, making things visible, getting the mapping between controls and results right, exploiting the power of constraints, and designing for error — assuming users will make mistakes and building in forgiveness rather than punishing them.

The book's analytical core is the **seven stages of action**: forming a goal, forming an intention, specifying an action, executing it, then perceiving the state of the world, interpreting it, and evaluating the outcome. The "gulf of execution" is the distance between what a user intends and what the object allows them to do; the "gulf of evaluation" is the distance between the object's state and what the user can perceive. Good design closes both gulfs.

Norman's famous example is the door: an unmarked door with handles on both sides causes people to pull when they should push, not because they are stupid but because the handle *signifies* pulling. The fix is not a label saying "PUSH" but a design — a flat push-plate — that makes the wrong action physically unnatural. The book turned "don't blame the user" from an apology into an engineering discipline and remains the standard introduction to human factors and product design.
