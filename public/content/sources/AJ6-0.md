**Brinkmanship** is the practice of pushing a dangerous confrontation to the edge (the "brink") of active conflict to force an opponent to concede rather than risk catastrophe. The term was attached to John Foster Dulles's Cold War strategy of "massive retaliation"; Dulles called it "the ability to get to the verge without getting into the war" — "the necessary art." Bertrand Russell compared nuclear brinkmanship to the teenage game of **chicken**: two drivers speed toward each other; the first to swerve loses, but if neither swerves both die.

Thomas Schelling's *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960) turned this into game theory. In chicken, pure rationality is a liability: the player who can *credibly commit* to not swerving wins — paradoxically, the player who visibly throws away the steering wheel forces the other to swerve. This is the **commitment tactic**: constraining your own freedom of action can increase your bargaining power, because a threat you cannot retreat from is a threat your opponent must take seriously. Credibility, not capability, is the scarce resource — and it is achieved by arranging incentives, burning bridges, delegating to uncontrollable agents, or making retreat politically impossible.

Schelling's subtler contribution is the **threat that leaves something to chance**. In nuclear crises you cannot credibly threaten deliberate all-out war (it is irrational to carry out); but you can create a situation where the risk of war *slips out of anyone's full control* — mobilizations, patrols, escalatory moves that make an accident or spiral possible — and then use that shared risk as coercive pressure. The opponent is not asked to believe you will choose disaster; only that disaster becomes more probable the longer they hold out. Brinkmanship is thus a competition in risk-taking, a "war of nerves" where the side more willing to tolerate catastrophe extracts concessions.

The Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) is the canonical case study: Kennedy and Khrushchev both faced mutual assured destruction, and the resolution — a secret trade of U.S. missiles in Turkey for Soviet missiles in Cuba — illustrates Schelling's other themes: the focal point (obvious, mutually recognized outcomes coordinate expectations even without communication), the importance of giving the opponent a face-saving exit, and the danger that neither side fully controls escalation (the near-war incidents unknown to leaders at the time).

The model generalizes to negotiations, lawsuits, labor disputes, and startup standoffs: high-stakes bargaining is often not about who is stronger but who can make threats credible, who can tolerate risk, and who structures the game so the other party bears the onus of backing down. The permanent warning is that games of chicken have an equilibrium where both sides refuse to yield — and that manipulators of risk can lose control of it.
