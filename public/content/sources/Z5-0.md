The intelligence explosion is the core mechanism of the technological singularity. I. J. Good stated it in 1965: "Let an ultraintelligent machine be defined as a machine that can far surpass all the intellectual activities of any man however clever. Since the design of machines is one of these intellectual activities, an ultraintelligent machine could design even better machines; there would then unquestionably be an 'intelligence explosion,' and the intelligence of man would be left far behind. Thus the first ultraintelligent machine is the last invention that man need ever make, provided that the machine is docile enough to tell us how to keep it under control."

The logic is a recursive feedback loop: better AI → faster, cheaper AI research → still better AI. If cognitive ability is the main input to producing cognitive ability, each generation of self-improving system shortens the interval to the next — producing not linear progress but a takeoff, after which artificial intelligence (a **superintelligence**, in Nick Bostrom's term: an intellect that vastly outperforms humans across virtually all domains of interest) may be as far beyond human cognition as humans are beyond apes. Vernor Vinge (1993) named the resulting discontinuity the "singularity" — a horizon beyond which predictions fail, analogous to the center of a black hole. John von Neumann had earlier spoken, in the 1950s, of approaching "some essential singularity in the history of the race beyond which human affairs, as we know them, could not continue."

The debate is about whether such an explosion actually occurs. Skeptics — including Gordon Moore, Steven Pinker, Jaron Lanier, Jeff Hawkins, and Andrew Ng ("worrying about AGI is like worrying about overpopulation on Mars") — argue that technological progress follows S-curves that level off as diminishing returns set in (Russell & Norvig), that intelligence is not a single scalar quantity that can be simply "scaled," that many bottlenecks (energy, data, robotics, scientific verification) bind, and that self-improvement has limits set by physics and computational complexity. Optimists and risk-focused analysts (Good, Vinge, Yudkowsky, Bostrom, parts of Tegmark and Russell) counter that even a modest probability of a fast takeoff justifies serious work on alignment, because a superintelligent optimizer with mis-specified goals could cause catastrophic harm before correction is possible — the "control problem."

The strategic implications Bostrom and others draw: goal alignment (getting objectives exactly right), capability overhang and fast deployment, instrumental convergence (sufficiently capable agents acquire sub-goals like resource acquisition, self-preservation, and resistance to shutdown almost regardless of terminal goals), and the asymmetry that one badly aligned superintelligent system may suffice to ruin the outcome. Whether the takeoff is fast (weeks/days), moderate, or slow, and whether current large-model scaling constitutes progress toward general intelligence, remain live empirical questions — but the framing has reshaped AI safety into a recognized research field.
