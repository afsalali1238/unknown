Charles T. Munger (1924–2023), vice chairman of Berkshire Hathaway and Warren Buffett's partner, became the chief popularizer of the **mental models** approach to decisions — building a latticework of the big ideas from across disciplines and applying them rather than relying on the narrow toolkit of one profession.

The core principles of his approach, as collected across the Farnam Street resources (his USC and Harvard School talks, Poor Charlie's Almanack, Berkshire essays):

- **Multiple models across disciplines.** Math (probability, compounding), accounting with its limits, psychology's biases, microeconomics (incentives, scale, competitive moats), and engineering (margin of safety). ~80–90 models carry most of the weight; avoid the "man with a hammer" syndrome of applying one lens to everything.
- **Inversion.** Solve problems backward: identify what guarantees failure, then avoid it; "tell me where I'll die so I never go there."
- **Avoid stupidity over seeking brilliance.** The checklist of what not to do (leverage without liquidity, lying, drifting, bad partners, ideological commitments) is a better life strategy than genius.
- **Circle of competence.** Know the edge of what you understand; operate inside it; extend it slowly.
- **Margin of safety and patience.** Bet rarely and big when the odds are overwhelming ("fat pitch" discipline); avoid situations where ruin is possible — long-term survival compounds.
- **Incentives rule.** "Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome."
- **Lifelong self-education.** Reading constantly, sleeping with books; Munger famously said he knew no wise people who didn't read all the time.
- **Quality over cigar-butts.** Munger's influence shifted Berkshire from cheap mediocre businesses to fair prices for wonderful businesses, with great management.

The Farnam Street curation serves as the index to the talks and letters — worldly wisdom as a *compounding* practice: acquire models, use them on every decision, and over decades the lattice becomes the decisive advantage over people who were never taught to think across the grain of academic and professional specialization.
