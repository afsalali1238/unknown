A mirror neuron is a neuron that fires both when an animal acts and when the animal observes the same action performed by another. The neuron "mirrors" the other's behavior as though the observer were itself acting. Such neurons have been directly observed in humans, other primates, and some bird species. In humans, brain activity consistent with mirror neurons has been found in the premotor cortex, the supplementary motor area, the primary somatosensory cortex, and the inferior parietal cortex.

The discovery came from the laboratory of Giacomo Rizzolatti at the University of Parma in the late 1980s and early 1990s. Recording from single neurons in the ventral premotor cortex of macaque monkeys during hand and mouth actions, the team — Luciano Fadiga, Leonardo Fogassi, Vittorio Gallese, and others — noticed that some neurons responded not only when the monkey itself reached for food but when it watched an experimenter reach for food. The first report was rejected by *Nature* for "lack of general interest" before being published elsewhere; roughly 10% of neurons in the monkey's inferior frontal and inferior parietal cortex turned out to have mirror properties, and the system was later shown to respond also to the *sound* of an action.

The mirror system's larger significance has been fiercely debated. Rizzolatti, Gallese, and Marco Iacoboni proposed that these cells are the neural basis of action understanding: to interpret what someone else is doing, your brain runs a simulation of the same action in your motor system. The same mechanism has been offered as a basis for empathy — you understand another person's feelings by partially re-enacting them — and for language, since Broca's area in humans appears homologous to the monkey's mirror region, suggesting communication may have evolved from motor imitation.

The claims are contested, however. Critics including Gregory Hickok and Cecilia Heyes argue that mirror neuron activity may be a *product* of learning and association rather than the engine of understanding, and that the strong versions — mirror neurons as the dedicated basis of empathy, autism, or language — run far ahead of the evidence. No single accepted neural or computational model yet explains how mirror activity supports cognition. A 2014 special issue of the *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* was devoted entirely to the controversy.

The durable insight regardless of resolution is that perception and action share machinery: watching an action engages the same circuits that produce it. Imitation, learning by watching, contagious yawning, the flinch you feel at someone else's fall — all suggest the brain does not treat other people's bodies as external objects to be reasoned about, but as patterns its own motor systems resonate with. Understanding others is, in part, something the body does.
