> Originally by Marc Andreessen in The Wall Street Journal, August 2011.
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the author.

Written at the crest of post-2008 panic about tech valuations, Marc Andreessen's essay argued that software companies were not a speculative bubble but the takeover of the entire economy: **software is eating the world**.

The empirical case: every industry was being rebuilt on software and delivered as digital services. Borders and Barnes & Noble were being displaced by Amazon (a software company with giant logistical engines); Netflix ate video distribution and then production; Spotify and iTunes ate music; the largest bookseller, music retailer, video service, and telecom-adjacent platforms were software companies. Photography (Shutterfly, Flickr, Instagram), telecom (Skype), recruiting (LinkedIn), and recruiting-adjacent services moved to software. Even "physical" industries became software companies with regulated atoms attached: modern agriculture runs on logistics software, oil and gas discovery runs on simulation, cars are software-laden computers, and the leading companies in each sector increasingly employed the talent and equity structures of Silicon Valley rather than those of the old industry.

Why software wins structurally: the marginal cost of delivering software is near zero, so once a digital product exists it can scale without proportional cost; software companies can iterate faster, serve long-tail markets, and attract top technical talent because they offer equity upside and engineering cultures that physical industries cannot match; and network effects and data accumulation create widening moats. Recessions and panic, Andreessen argued, are when this accelerates: incumbents starve their technology budgets while software companies hire and build. The risk to the U.S. economy, he insisted, was not tech overvaluation but that incumbents and regulators would fail to adapt, ceding software leadership to other nations.

The essay's broader predictions have aged well beyond its examples: the largest companies in the world became software/platform companies; "every company becomes a software company" became standard strategy; the mobile wave (covered in "Mobile Is Eating the World") extended the same logic; AI in the 2020s deepened it by making software itself faster to produce. Critiques that emerged later: the essay underweighted the political and social costs — labor disruption, platform monopolies, attention extraction, housing and inequality effects — and software's appetite for regulated industries sometimes produces fragility and externalities rather than consumer benefit. As a strategy document its durable instructions are for founders to attack incumbents' software-vulnerable margins, for incumbents to treat technology capability as core rather than back-office, and for investors to notice that industries not yet "eaten" (health, education, finance, government) are the opportunity set. The phrase remains the one-line summary of how a marginal-cost-zero technology restructures a cost-based economy.
