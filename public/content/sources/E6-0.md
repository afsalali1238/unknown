The capital asset pricing model (CAPM) is a finance model used to determine the theoretically appropriate required rate of return of an asset, given its risk, for decisions about adding it to a well-diversified portfolio. It was developed independently by William Sharpe (1964), John Lintner (1965), Jan Mossin (1966), and Jack Treynor (unpublished, 1961–62), building on Harry Markowitz's modern portfolio theory. Sharpe, Markowitz, and Merton Miller shared the 1990 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for this body of work.

CAPM's central insight separates two kinds of risk. **Unsystematic** (idiosyncratic) risk is risk specific to a single company — its management, its products, its factories. This risk can be diversified away: hold enough different stocks and the surprises cancel, so the market does not reward you for bearing it. **Systematic** risk is risk that cannot be diversified away — the exposure of the asset to the market as a whole. The market only rewards risk you cannot eliminate.

Systematic risk is measured by **beta (β)**: the sensitivity of the asset's returns to movements in the overall market. A stock with beta of 1.5 tends to rise or fall 1.5% when the market moves 1%; a stock with beta 0.5 moves half as much. The CAPM formula says the expected return of an asset equals the risk-free rate plus beta times the market risk premium: expected return = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf). Treasury bills set the floor (time value of money); the extra return is proportional only to beta.

Graphically this is the **security market line (SML)**: expected return on the vertical axis, beta on the horizontal. In equilibrium every asset should plot on the line. An asset above the line offers more return than its systematic risk justifies — it is undervalued; below the line, overvalued. The model assumes investors are rational and diversified, can borrow and lend at the risk-free rate, face no transaction costs, and agree on expected returns — assumptions strong enough that empirical tests have often failed: low-beta stocks, value stocks, and small stocks have earned higher returns than their measured betas predict.

Fischer Black's zero-beta CAPM (1972) relaxed the risk-free borrowing assumption and proved more robust. Despite its empirical failings and the rise of multifactor models (Fama–French three-factor, arbitrage pricing theory), CAPM survives because it captures a truth that survives its simplifications: diversification is free, the market does not pay for risk it can eliminate, and the return you can demand is proportional to how an investment moves *with everything else you own*.
