The law of large numbers is the probability-theory result that the average of a large number of independent, identically distributed samples converges to the true expected value — the population mean — as the number of samples grows. Roll one fair six-sided die and the result can be anything from 1 to 6; the expected value is 3.5, but no single roll gives 3.5. Roll the die a thousand times, and the average of all rolls will be close to 3.5; roll it a million times and it will be closer still.

The law guarantees stable long-run results for averages of random events. A casino may lose money on a single spin of roulette, but over a large number of spins its earnings approach a predictable percentage — the house edge — and any individual winning streak is eventually overcome by the game's parameters. Insurance works on the same basis: one customer's losses are wildly unpredictable, but the average loss across a hundred thousand policyholders is close to knowable in advance. The empirical frequency of success in repeated Bernoulli trials — coin flips, conversions, survival rates — converges to the theoretical probability as the trials pile up; Monte Carlo simulation exploits this by estimating answers to hard problems through massive random sampling.

There are two versions. The **weak law** (proved by Jakob Bernoulli around 1689 for the case of independent binary trials, published posthumously in *Ars Conjectandi*, 1713) states that the sample average converges *in probability* to the mean — for any chosen margin of error, the probability of the average falling within that margin approaches one. The **strong law** states that the sample average converges *almost surely* — with probability one, it will eventually stay arbitrarily close to the mean forever. The distinction is technical but reflects how thoroughly mathematics justified the intuition that frequency reveals probability.

The two pitfalls the law warns against are as important as the law itself. First, it applies only to *large* numbers: there is no principle that a small number of observations will match the expected value — a run of good or bad early results is the norm, not a violation. Second, and more famously, the law does *not* say that streaks are "balanced out" in the short run — that is the gambler's fallacy. The proportion of heads approaches one-half as coins are flipped, but the absolute difference between heads and tails typically grows; randomness converges by dilution, not by correction. Ten reds in a row at roulette do not make black due — the wheel has no memory, and the law of large numbers works precisely because future spins don't adjust to repay the past. A further limitation: without finite expectation (heavy-tailed distributions like the Cauchy, or some models of financial returns), the average fails to converge at all — which is why the law's guarantee depends on conditions that real-world "obvious" statistics sometimes violate.
