> Archived 2026-09-06 as an excerpt for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the original author.

This essay is a response to Scott Alexander's *Meditations on Moloch*, which lays out fourteen scenarios where participants get trapped in bad equilibria: fishermen who fail to coordinate on filters that would benefit everyone; a Malthusian rat race where only those who do nothing but compete survive; perfectly competitive markets that cannot pay a living wage; schools tied to land prices that funnel income to landowners; arms races ("if you want peace, prepare for war"); cancer cells that multiply and kill the host; governments competing to offer bigger bribes to lure business; education as a pure signaling competition; science whose procedures fail so that most findings aren't real; and so on.

Alexander's argument, as Zvi Mowshowitz summarizes it, is that under *perfect* competition, value gets wiped out: competitors willing to sacrifice more of what they value in order to win will eventually outlast those who don't. When you optimize for X and are indifferent to Y, you by default optimize *against* Y — and anything humans value (Y) makes a claim on resources, so it comes under pressure. We might live in a temporary "dream time" of surplus resources, but in the steady state, horrible things become the most efficient things, and "every simple optimization target yet suggested would, if fully implemented, destroy all value in the universe." The only good escape, in that framing, is coordination.

Zvi's reply is the title: **Moloch hasn't won.** It's easy to forget that the world we live in does not actually work the way the gloomy model predicts. Kingdoms don't reliably expand to their breaking points. Poisons don't keep making their way into the coffee. Iterated prisoner's dilemmas often *succeed* — people cooperate. Dollar auctions are not all over the internet. Most corporations do not grind their workers to dust even in competitive industries.

The explanation, Zvi argues, is that the real world rarely presents the *perfect* competition the model requires. Perfect competition assumes perfectly rational agents, perfect information, continuous-time strategies, and no slack. Real competition is riddled with imperfections: physical constraints (working people to death is inefficient, even for slave owners), excess resources that buy temporary respite, the fact that the things competitions maximize for are often things we actually like, and — crucially — slack. Many of the worst "Molochian" outcomes, like the dysfunctional corporations he calls "immoral mazes," are products not of too much competition but of *too little*: organizations insulated from market pressure, where signaling and office politics replace the discipline of serving customers.

The practical upshot is hopeful but conditional. The forces that grind value out of systems are real, but they are not sovereign: they fail constantly, wherever competition is imperfect, wherever people retain slack, and wherever coordination exists. The most vital part of *Meditations on Moloch*, Zvi concludes, is the hope — "that we are winning. Yes, there are abominations and super-powerful forces out there looking to eat us and destroy everything of value, and yet we still have lots of stuff that has value." The job is to notice where the model does and doesn't apply, defend the slack and coordination that keep Moloch at bay, and not to mistake a tendency for a verdict.
