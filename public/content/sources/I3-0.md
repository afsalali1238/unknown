> Originally by Benedict Evans; annual "Mobile Is Eating the World" presentations, 2014–2019.
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the author.

Benedict Evans's "Mobile Is Eating the World" presentations (produced annually at Andreessen Horowitz from 2014) updated Andreessen's "software eats the world" with the next layer: the smartphone had made computing personal, ubiquitous, and global, and the mobile wave was bigger — and structurally different — than the PC wave that preceded it.

The scale argument: smartphones were not merely phones with internet; they were personal supercomputers sold to every adult on earth. The installed base went from ~1.5 billion PCs (built over 30 years, concentrated in offices and rich-world homes) to ~5 billion smartphones (built in under a decade, carried by teenagers, farmers, shopkeepers, and gig workers worldwide). For the first time, the dominant computing platform reached almost everyone, all the time, with sensors (GPS, camera, microphone, accelerometer), identity (SIM/billing), payment rails, and location attached. The PC internet gave you the web when you sat down; mobile put a networked computer in the flow of life.

The structural consequences Evans documented: tech giants of the PC era were challenged — Google, Facebook (and later Instagram, WeChat, TikTok) were mobile-native; Microsoft nearly missed the transition; Apple took the largest share of industry profits. Value moved to apps, messaging, and super-apps (WeChat in China became identity, payments, commerce, and services in one). E-commerce shifted from desktop sessions to impulse purchases and rides; new categories became possible because the device was always there: on-demand services (Uber, DoorDash), mobile money (M-Pesa in Kenya leapfrogging banks), photography as a default feature rather than an industry, navigation as a free utility, and advertising rebuilt around attention and identity.

The framework Evans pushed was S-curve thinking plus platform transitions: technology adoption follows curves; when a new computing platform arrives (mainframe → mini → PC → web → mobile → AI), the leaders of the previous wave are often displaced because their business models, distribution, and organizations are optimized for the old curve. Incumbents tend to treat the new platform as a port of the old one (mobile websites) rather than rebuilding for its native properties (camera-first, location, notifications, touch, small sessions). The business advice implied: watch unit sales and developer attention for where the new curve is; expect new winners; and ask what becomes possible only on the new device.

Retrospectively the series correctly identified the shift, with the same blind spot as the earlier wave — underweighting the political fallout (platform power, attention economy, antitrust). Evans's later work extended the same method to AI as the next platform transition. The reusable lesson is the pattern-recognition: when computing form-factor changes, distribution, moats, and winners reset — and the early, toy-looking versions of the new platform (first phones' bad web browsers) are where the incumbents look away until too late.
