The narrative fallacy (Taleb) is our compulsion to compress random, complex reality into coherent stories — and then mistake the story for the mechanism. Stories impose a beginning, a small number of actors, causal links, and a point; the world has vast numbers of actors, luck, feedback loops, and no moral.

Why the brain does it: stories are memory-compression devices; we recall patterns with human actors and emotional arcs far better than statistics. But compression has costs: after outcomes are known, history looks inevitable (hindsight bias), luck is assigned as skill or failure, and the role of silence — failures that don't write books or give interviews (survivorship bias) — disappears. Biographies of billionaires extract the habits of people who succeeded where thousands with identical habits are absent from the sample; market commentary explains every day's move as if caused by the headline attached to it.

For decision-making the fallacy matters because stories that fit the past beautifully often fail out-of-sample. The defense: prefer base rates and distributions to single anecdotes; actively seek the failures and the un-famous; separate storytelling ("what happened") from mechanism ("what would predict it"); run decision journals so you can compare your before-the-fact reasoning to outcomes rather than rewriting memory; and remember that convincing yourself is easiest of all. Taleb's own caveat applies to this note: humans cannot drop narrative — the goal is holding the story lightly and keeping the un-story evidence visible around it.
