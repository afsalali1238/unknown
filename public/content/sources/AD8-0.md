Epigenetics is the study of changes in gene expression that occur without altering the DNA sequence. The Greek prefix *epi-* ("over, outside of, around") implies features that are "on top of" or "in addition to" the traditional DNA-sequence mechanism of inheritance. Epigenetic changes usually persist through cell divisions and affect the regulation of gene expression; they may result from environmental factors or be part of normal development.

The chief mechanisms are DNA methylation and histone modification, each of which alters how genes are expressed without changing the underlying sequence. In methylation, chemical tags attach to DNA and tend to silence the tagged genes; in histone modification, proteins around which DNA is wrapped are chemically altered, making stretches of the genome more or less accessible for transcription. Non-coding RNA molecules also play a key regulatory role. These changes may last through cell divisions for the life of the cell, and some persist across multiple generations — even though they involve no change to the nucleotide sequence.

The most familiar example is cellular differentiation. A single fertilized egg — the zygote — divides into daughter cells that become neurons, muscle cells, epithelium, blood-vessel endothelium, and so on. Every one of these cells carries the same DNA; what makes them different is that epigenetic marks activate some genes and silence others. The British embryologist C. H. Waddington coined the term in 1942, imagining development as a ball rolling down an "epigenetic landscape" of ridges and valleys: once a cell rolls into a valley — a lineage — it stays there, even though all cells started from the same hilltop.

The reason epigenetics matters beyond developmental biology is that the marks are responsive to the environment. Diet, stress, toxins, and behavior can alter methylation patterns, changing which genes are expressed; some of these alterations have been shown to pass from parents to offspring in animal models. This is the molecular basis of the long-running argument about "nature versus nurture": the genome is the piano, but epigenetics decides which keys get played, and experience can retune the instrument.

A 2008 Cold Spring Harbor meeting defined an epigenetic trait as a "stably heritable phenotype resulting from changes in a chromosome without alterations in the DNA sequence." The field has since reshaped how biology thinks about inheritance, cancer (where abnormal methylation silences tumor-suppressor genes), aging, and the boundary between environmental exposure and biological fate.
