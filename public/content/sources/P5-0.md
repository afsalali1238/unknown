In his interview with Shane Parrish, Naval Ravikant describes how reading became his dominant habit and how he recommends others build it. He grew up a latchkey kid in New York; the library was his after-school center, and he read anything available — comic books, Reader's Digests, mysteries — because nobody forced a syllabus on him. His headline advice: **read what you love until you love to read.**

Key recommendations from his comments on reading:

- **Start with genuine interest, not duty.** Forced reading (classics you "should" read before you care) kills the habit. Read whatever genuinely pulls you — junk included — because volume and pleasure build the reading reflex; taste self-upgrades toward harder material over time. The goal of a reading habit is to become someone who reads constantly, not to complete an approved list.
- **Read widely and re-read the fundamentals.** Most of his reading is math, science, philosophy — "the classics, whatever you want to call the old books" — because old, still-reprinted books have passed a heavy selection filter: ideas that survive a century are more valuable than last week's news. Re-reading is a sign: books that repay re-reads are the ones worth marking up.
- **Read for understanding, not completion.** It is fine to abandon books. Starting many books and finishing few ("quit early, quit often") beats dutiful slogging; the cost of dropping a bad book is low, and the cost of training yourself to associate reading with chores is high. Speed matters less than retention and application.
- **Build a foundation, then cross-reference.** He describes mental models as his approach — reading the great books across fields (science, math, philosophy, psychology) so knowledge compounds; when one concept appears in multiple sources it becomes permanent.
- **Make reading the default activity.** His other advice treats attention as the scarce resource: replace the phone-in-every-gap moment with a book; if reading is the most convenient option available, reading wins. Discipline as environment design rather than willpower.

The philosophy underneath: reading is the highest-leverage habit because it downloads decades of others' experience into your head cheaply, and because in an age of abundant media, deliberately chosen long-form reading selects against manipulation by feeds and urgency. "The person who reads too much and uses his own brain too little falls into lazy habits of thinking" is the counterweight — reading plus re-deriving ideas for yourself is the compounding loop. The same episode covers decision-making, habits, and values, all tied together by the idea that inputs (books, people, environment) dominate long-run outcomes.
