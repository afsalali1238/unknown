The map of reality is not reality. Even the best maps are imperfect. That's because maps are reductions of what they represent. If a map were to represent the territory with perfect fidelity, it would no longer be a reduction and thus would no longer be useful to us. A map can also be a snapshot from a point in time, representing something that no longer exists.

We use maps every day to simplify complexity. A company's financial statements distill thousands of transactions into something manageable — yet they tell us nothing about whether the product is good for customers or what's really going on in the company. A policy document, a parenting manual, a performance review — all are models, or maps, that simplify some complex territory to guide us through it.

Relying solely on maps can lead you to the wrong conclusion. You need to touch the territory. Very early in Amazon's history, Jeff Bezos was going over documents at the weekly business review. He'd heard customers complaining (the territory) about call wait times, yet the data (the map) showed wait times under sixty seconds. Bezos paused the meeting, picked up the phone, dialed the 1-800 number, and waited on hold for over ten minutes — making the point that something was wrong with the data collection. "When the data and the anecdotes disagree," he said, "the anecdotes are usually right."

## But my GPS didn't show that cliff

We need maps and models as guides. But frequently we forget that they are abstractions, and fail to understand their limits. We run into problems when our knowledge becomes knowledge of the *map* rather than of the actual underlying territory. When we mistake the map for the territory, we start to think we have all the answers; we create static rules that deal with the map while forgetting we exist in a constantly changing world. When we close off feedback loops, we don't see that the terrain has changed.

Maps and models are not meant to live forever as static references. If reality has changed, the map must change. Take Newtonian physics: for hundreds of years it was an extremely useful map. Then Einstein's relativity replaced part of it. Newtonian physics is still reliably useful for predicting the movement of large objects — but physicists carefully delimit where it applies, and when they hit uncharted territory like quantum mechanics, they explore instead of assuming their existing maps explain everything.

Some of the biggest map/territory problems are risks that are not shown on the map at all. We do not understand a model or reduction unless we understand and respect its limitations. To use a map as accurately as possible, take into account three principles:

**1. Reality is the ultimate update.** Territories change, sometimes faster than the maps describing them. Good maps are built through feedback loops created by explorers — but a map captures a territory at a moment in time, and the faster the rate of change, the harder it is to keep up to date.

**2. Consider the cartographer.** Maps are not purely objective. They reflect the values, standards, and limitations of their creators. Models are most useful when considered in the context in which they were created: what was the cartographer trying to achieve, and how does that shape what's depicted?

**3. Maps can influence territories.** This was central to Jane Jacobs's *The Death and Life of Great American Cities*: city planners imposed elaborate models on cities without paying attention to how cities actually lived, and the models then reshaped — and damaged — the territory they purported to describe.

Mental models are maps. While they might not be perfectly accurate, they are useful — to the extent they are explanatory and predictive, and to the extent we keep touching the territory.
