Russell's paradox is a contradiction — a logical impossibility — of concern to the foundations of set theory and to logical reasoning generally. It was discovered by Bertrand Russell in or around 1901. In a letter to Gottlob Frege, Russell outlined the problem as it affected Frege's major work, the *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik*. Frege responded with both dismay and admiration, and never fully recovered from the blow to his life's work. Russell was himself alarmed by how deeply the paradox threatened his own project; struggling to solve it during years of intensive work and emotional upheaval, he made some of his most important discoveries, including his theory of denoting phrases and his theory of types.

The paradox arises from a principle at the heart of naive set theory: the unrestricted comprehension principle, which says that for any well-defined property φ(x), there exists a set {x : φ(x)} whose members are exactly the things satisfying φ. Let φ(x) stand for "x is not a member of itself," and let R be the set of all sets that are not members of themselves: R = {x : x ∉ x}.

Now ask: is R a member of itself? If R *is* a member of itself, then it must satisfy its own defining condition — not being a member of itself — so it is not. If R is *not* a member of itself, then it satisfies the condition and must be a member. Each answer implies its opposite. Contradiction.

The popular version is the village barber who shaves all and only villagers who do not shave themselves: does the barber shave himself? If he does, he doesn't; if he doesn't, he does. But as the SEP notes, the set-theoretic version is deeper than the barber puzzle, because the barber can simply not exist, whereas naive set theory *promised* that R must exist. The reasoning is also remarkably general: it does not require any specifically set-theoretic principle beyond the thought that a relation might hold of all and only the things to which it does not apply — it is an instance of the pure-logical theorem that there is no y such that for all x, Fxy if and only if not-Fxx.

The paradox reshaped twentieth-century logic. Russell's own response was the theory of types: entities form a hierarchy, and a set can only have members of the type below it, so "x ∈ x" is not false but meaningless — the question cannot be asked. Ernst Zermelo (who had noticed a similar contradiction independently) axiomatized set theory so that comprehension is restricted: you can form subsets of existing sets satisfying a condition, but not a set out of anything satisfying a condition. David Hilbert and John von Neumann developed further foundational programs in response. Jean van Heijenoort wrote that "Russell's paradox has been leaven in modern logic" — a small contradiction that made an entire science of foundations rise.

The lesson generalizes beyond mathematics. Any system that allows self-reference of an unrestricted kind risks generating contradictions: the set of all sets not containing themselves, the library catalogue of all catalogues that don't list themselves, the Cretan who says "all Cretans are liars." Russell's paradox is the original warning that a powerful enough rule applied to itself can explode.
