Tim Wu's *The Attention Merchants: The Epic Scramble to Get Inside Our Heads* (2016) traces the business model of harvesting human attention and selling it to advertisers — from the first mass-circulation penny newspapers, through radio and television, to the internet and the smartphone. Wikipedia's article on the attention economy provides the conceptual frame: human attention is treated as a scarce commodity in an information-saturated world.

The foundational observation is Herbert Simon's (1971): "A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention." Information consumes the attention of its recipients; as information supply explodes, the binding constraint on producers is not distribution or even content but capture of human focus. Davenport and Beck define attention as "focused mental engagement on a particular item of information" — items enter awareness, attention selects one, and then action may follow.

The attention-merchant business model runs on a three-step loop: (1) gather an audience with free or cheap content; (2) measure and hold their attention; (3) sell access to that attention to advertisers. The customer for the business is the advertiser; the audience is the product. Wu's historical chapters show the model's repetitions: Benjamin Day's *New York Sun* (1833) sold papers below cost to capture eyes; patent-medicine advertising funded the press; radio gave free entertainment in exchange for exposure; television engineered prime time around the attention span; online media then added engagement optimization — the feed, infinite scroll, notifications — and surveillance-based targeting, making attention capture industrial, personalized, and continuous.

Each cycle produces a counter-wave of resistance — ad-avoidance, regulation, "attention diets" — followed by new capture techniques that push further into everyday life. Wu emphasizes that attention capture is zero-sum in a deep sense: focus given to one thing is withheld from everything else, including the user's own purposes. The model therefore generates predictable pathologies: outrage and novelty outcompete nuance (they hold attention); engagement metrics drive content design rather than user benefit; persuasion engineered for children or emotional states crosses ethical lines; and the most successful platforms become "gilded cages" — pleasurable places designed to keep users inside.

The framing connects to agenda-setting, filter bubbles, propaganda models, and behavioral design. For individuals the operational lesson is to treat attention as the primary scarce resource and to audit who is harvesting it; for builders, the reminder that if a product is "free," the monetized commodity is likely the audience's focus, and the design incentives follow from that.
