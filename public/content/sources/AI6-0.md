The iron law of oligarchy is a political theory first developed by the German-born Italian sociologist Robert Michels in his 1911 book *Political Parties*. It asserts that rule by an elite — oligarchy — is inevitable within any democratic organization, as an "iron law" arising from the "tactical and technical necessities" of organization itself.

Michels had observed that the socialist parties of Europe, despite their democratic ideology and formal provisions for mass participation, were dominated by their leaders just as thoroughly as conservative parties were. His conclusion was that the problem lay not in the parties' ideals but in the nature of organizations. As complexity grows, direct democracy becomes impossible; power is necessarily delegated to individuals. In Michels's most famous formulation: "It is organization which gives dominion of the elected over the electors.... Who says organization, says oligarchy."

The mechanism, as summarized by sociologist Darcy K. Leach, is stark: "Bureaucracy happens. If bureaucracy happens, power rises. Power corrupts." Any large organization must create a bureaucracy to function — thousands or hundreds of thousands of members cannot make daily decisions directly. Delegation leads to specialization: the leadership develops knowledge, skills, information channels, and resources that the rank and file lack. That specialization in turn alienates the leadership from the membership and entrenches the leaders in office. Meanwhile, members feel a psychological need to be led, and leaders possess "the desire to dominate," which Michels called a universal elementary psychological fact. The result is that even parties founded to overthrow hierarchy reproduce it internally.

Michels used the German Social Democratic Party and European trade unions as his chief examples — organizations explicitly committed to equality that nonetheless became controlled by permanent leadership classes. He drew the pessimistic conclusion that "historical evolution mocks all the prophylactic measures that have been adopted for the prevention of oligarchy."

Later social scientists have tested the law against counterexamples — small democratic federations, rotating-office systems like the early Israeli kibbutzim, and organizations with strong recall mechanisms — finding that oligarchy can be slowed or resisted when leadership rotation, information transparency, and membership participation are enforced, but that scale and age push almost every organization toward it. The theory remains a core reference in the sociology of organizations, labor unions, political parties, and (in later applications) corporations and online communities.
