Selectorate theory, first formalized in *The Logic of Political Survival* (Bueno de Mesquita, Smith, Siverson & Morrow) and popularized in *The Dictator's Handbook* (2011), explains the behavior of leaders — democratic, autocratic, corporate, or any organization with a top leader — from a single assumption: **the primary goal of a leader is to stay in power**. Policy choices are mostly instruments for that goal.

The model divides the population into nested groups:

- The **nominal selectorate** ("interchangeables"): everyone with any theoretical say in choosing the leader — all registered voters, or everyone eligible in the party.
- The **real selectorate** ("influentials"): those who actually participate or could realistically weigh in.
- The **winning coalition** ("essentials"): the subset whose support actually keeps the leader in office — the voters who produce a majority, or the generals, oligarchs, and security chiefs in an autocracy.

A leader survives by keeping the winning coalition loyal. The crucial variable is the ratio W/S (winning coalition to selectorate), the "loyalty norm." With a **small winning coalition** (autocracy, many family firms), leaders stay in power with **private goods**: cash, privileges, monopolies, and corruption targeted at a few essential supporters. Keeping supporters dependent matters — a large selectorate and small coalition means each coalition member is easily replaceable, so they stay loyal cheaply. With a **large winning coalition** (democracy, public-company boards with dispersed power), buying individuals off is too expensive and risky, so leaders must deliver **public goods** — growth, health, security, clean institutions — that benefit everyone.

Bueno de Mesquita and Smith distill five rules for rulers:

1. Shrink the number of essential supporters (or keep them small) — fewer people to satisfy.
2. Keep the selectorate large — so insiders can be replaced if they defect.
3. Extract as much revenue as possible without provoking rebellion or collapse.
4. Pay the winning coalition just enough to keep them loyal — never too much, or they become independent threats.
5. Spend the remainder at your own discretion — but don't let supporters get autonomous wealth.

The theory's payoff is unified predictions: autocrats underprovide public goods, hate transparency, tolerate corruption, and start wars they can win cheaply (victory buys private revenue); democrats fight harder and longer when wars start but pick them carefully; foreign aid tends to prop up coalition payments rather than development; leaders welcome crises that let them reconfigure coalitions. It also applies to CEOs and board politics, partnerships, and any system where a leader must satisfy a selectable group — making it a general model of political survival rather than a theory of "dictators" alone.
