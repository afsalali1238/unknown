Annie Duke, poker champion turned decision scientist, argues that decisions are bets on the future made under uncertainty — and that improving decisions means separating decision *quality* from outcome *quality*, which the world constantly conflates. Her books *Thinking in Bets* and *How to Decide* systematize the habits of professional gamblers.

Core ideas:

- **Resulting.** We judge decisions by how they turn out: good outcomes feel like good decisions. But luck and hidden information mean a well-reasoned bet can lose and a terrible bet can win. Poker trains the correction: you must evaluate the quality of the decision given what was knowable at the time, while acknowledging that luck renders feedback noisy. In life the sample sizes per person are small and slow, so resulting is even more tempting and more misleading.
- **Probabilistic thinking.** Express beliefs as probabilities ("I'm about 70%") rather than certainties; this forces calibration, allows update, and removes the all-or-nothing defensiveness of identity-held opinions.
- **The premortem and "what could make me wrong?"** Imagining a future failure and working backward to its causes (Gary Klein's premortem) surfaces risks that planning forward hides; deciding in advance what evidence would change your mind builds honesty.
- **Truthseeking over winning arguments.** Duke emphasizes "dissolving the side" — groups committed to accuracy reward saying "I don't know," changing minds, and truth-telling, rather than punishing it; contrast the usual group dynamic where dissent is loss.
- **Tilt and emotional regulation.** Emotion after losses distorts subsequent decisions (chasing losses); routines that insert pause between result and next decision protect process.

The practical package: write down forecasts and check them; judge your year by decision quality (a learnable skill) rather than by realized luck; say the words "I'm not sure"; and build a "truthsaying" group — peers committed to accurate feedback over politeness. The poker insight is that uncertainty is not a defect in the decision environment; it is the environment — and the winning strategy is probabilistic humility plus honest feedback loops.
