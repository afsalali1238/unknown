Winner-take-all (or winner-take-most) markets are ones where small differences in performance translate into massive differences in reward, rather than proportionate pay. Robert Frank and Philip Cook's *The Winner-Take-All Society* documented the spread of these markets as technology made the best option globally accessible.

The mechanics: (1) **replication and distribution** — once the best performance can be reproduced or network-delivered at near-zero marginal cost, there is little reason to settle for second-best (top music, software, content, platforms); (2) **network effects** — the biggest network becomes more valuable to the next user; (3) **bounded slots** — a person or firm can only do business with one or a few providers, so rankings get amplified. The payoff distribution becomes a power law: rank 1 takes orders of magnitude more than rank 2, and the long tail shares scraps.

Implications: effort and reward decouple from proportionality; competition becomes positional — being great in absolute terms matters less than being *relatively* best at what others can access; over-entry into visible winner-take-all fields (everyone runs to the visible tournament) while unsexy proportional markets are neglected (counsel: choose local markets where you can be best or near-unique; in WTA markets, aim for a niche you can win); the median participant's expected value is terrible even when the average looks good — a portfolio thinking error, since the expected value is dominated by a few lottery winners; and tournaments distort effort and ethics (status striving, corruption).

Strategic lessons: identify which markets are WTA vs. proportionate — software, media, platforms, finance performance, social status tend WTA; local services, relationships, niche B2B, craftsmanship more often proportionate. In WTA fields either go for top with asymmetric effort or find the sub-niche where you *are* rank one; in proportional markets, steady and good is genuinely rewarded. For observers: beware career advice drawn only from tournament winners — invisible base rate failure; beware investors paying only for #1 stories — most will be #20.
