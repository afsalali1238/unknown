> House-curated overview of Sam Altman's "Moore's Law for Everything" (2021) and the debate it triggered.
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the cited authors.

Sam Altman's essay "Moore's Law for Everything" (March 2021) argued that within a generation AI will create enough abundance to make it possible for every person to have access to unprecedented wealth — and that the mechanism for distributing it should be **universal basic income (UBI) funded by taxing AI/land/capital**, or as he framed it, an "American Equity Fund" that distributes dividends of the automated economy to citizens.

The argument's steps: intelligence is the main input into production; AI can increasingly provide that intelligence as a cheap, abundant service; as this "intelligence price" trends toward zero, the cost of producing most goods and services — which are at root energy + intelligence + matter — should fall dramatically. The cost of energy is already falling with renewables; matter is limited but a small fraction of end-product cost. The likely outcome is that demand for human labor falls across most sectors, while the total pie explodes — concentrated ownership of AI means gains concentrate with the owners of capital and data, and a political/economic fix is needed.

Altman's proposal: UBI as dividend of the automated economy — the government taxes AI/land/energy or uses a sovereign wealth fund holding shares of AI companies, and distributes cash to all citizens unconditionally (above the safety net, replacing not healthcare/education); this keeps markets, individual choice, and incentive intact, avoids bureaucratic means-testing, and acknowledges common ownership of the underlying assets of civilization (legal system, infrastructure, prior science).

Critiques from both sides. On the left and among labor economists: cash alone in a world where winners set prices and housing/healthcare/education supply is constrained (driven by land and incumbent institutions) may be captured by rentiers — you need to fix supply (housing, health, schooling) or UBI becomes rent; also, why give the dividend to everyone including rich people rather than public goods, and is taxing the AI industry feasible when it is globally mobile? On the right and in tech-libertarian circles: governments and equity funds of the scale proposed are slow and corrupt; the historical track record of sovereign wealth funds distributing to citizens (Alaska's permanent fund being the small working example) shows modest returns; UBI trials (Finland, Stockton) showed well-being gains but not wholesale economic restructuring; and automation waves before (agriculture, manufacturing) transformed but did not eliminate work, with new categories replacing old — predictions of post-work have been wrong for 200 years. Other critics noted the oddity of the AI lab owner proposing a policy whose plausibility justified his own company's scale.

The lasting relevance: the essay is the clearest statement of the "AI abundance → redistribution" pipeline and set the terms for subsequent debates (data dividends, compute taxes, UBI trials). The unresolved core question remains empirical — how much of AI's surplus is labor-substituting vs. augmenting, and how fast — and distributional, which the essay made mainstream: if scarcity is increasingly political rather than technological, the central problem becomes distributing abundance without destroying the system producing it.
