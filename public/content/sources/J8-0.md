The Lorenz system is a set of three ordinary differential equations developed by meteorologist Edward Lorenz in 1963 as a simplified model of atmospheric convection — the way air moves when heated from below and cooled from above. It became the classic demonstration of **deterministic chaos**: a system with no randomness whatsoever, governed entirely by fixed rules, whose long-term behavior is nonetheless practically unpredictable.

The equations describe how three quantities change over time: x, proportional to the intensity of convection; y, proportional to the temperature difference between rising and falling currents; and z, proportional to the distortion of the vertical temperature profile. The constants σ, ρ, and β represent physical properties of the system. For certain parameter values — the famous σ = 10, ρ = 28, β = 8/3 — the system's solutions never settle into a steady state or a repeating cycle. They trace out the Lorenz attractor, a looping, double-lobed pattern famously resembling a butterfly, circling one lobe then switching unpredictably to the other, forever.

The discovery happened by accident. Lorenz had been running a computer weather simulation and, wanting to see a run again, re-entered the initial conditions from a printout that rounded the numbers to three decimal places instead of the machine's six. The tiny truncation difference — about one part in a thousand — produced a simulation that diverged completely from the original within a short time. In a deterministic system, identical inputs should give identical outputs; the rounding was nearly identical, yet the outputs were totally different. Small differences in initial conditions were being amplified exponentially.

This is the **butterfly effect** (the term comes from Lorenz's 1972 talk title, "Does the Flap of a Butterfly's Wings in Brazil Set Off a Tornado in Texas?"). The system is aperiodic — it never exactly repeats — and deterministic — given exact initial conditions, the future is mathematically fixed — but because physical measurements can never be exact, the practical predictability horizon is finite and short. Errors double roughly each cycle, so even perfect theory and very good data give useful forecasts only weeks ahead at most for weather.

The broader significance: chaos showed that unpredictability does not require randomness or free will. Simple deterministic rules involving nonlinear feedback (variables multiplying each other) can generate behavior of arbitrary apparent complexity — stable-looking systems like the weather, markets, ecologies, and even heartbeats can possess inherent horizons of prediction. Lorenz's work founded chaos theory alongside Mandelbrot's fractals, and reframed the scientific ambition for such systems: not precise long-term prediction, but understanding the structure of the attractors, invariants, and statistics that persist despite the chaos. Since 1963 the Lorenz equations have been found to model lasers, electrical circuits, dynamos, and chemical reactions — a single miniature machine for the mathematics of unpredictability.
