The Kelly criterion is a formula for sizing a sequence of bets so as to maximize the long-term growth rate of wealth — equivalently, to maximize the expected value of the logarithm of wealth. It was described in 1956 by John Larry Kelly Jr., a researcher at Bell Labs, in the paper "A New Interpretation of Information Rate." Kelly had been thinking about the problem of a gambler with inside information over a noisy telephone line: how much should he bet on each signal to grow his capital fastest without ever going broke?

For a simple binary bet — win with probability p, lose with probability q = 1−p, gaining g times the stake on a win and losing a fraction l of it on a loss — the Kelly fraction of current bankroll to wager is f = p/l − q/g. In the classic even-money case (g = l = 1), this simplifies to f = p − q = 2p − 1: bet your edge. If your probability of winning is 60%, bet 20% of your bankroll; if you have no edge (p = 50%), bet nothing; if the odds are against you, the formula says don't play.

The result rests on a mathematical fact: maximizing expected arithmetic return leads to ruin, while maximizing the expected *log* of wealth leads to compounding. A strategy that bets everything each round has the highest expected arithmetic return but almost surely goes broke; Kelly is the unique fraction that beats any other strategy in the long run, almost surely. Edward Thorp used Kelly to size bets in card-counting blackjack and later in convertible-arbitrage investing, and argued in *Beat the Dealer* and *A Man for All Markets* that it explained the position sizing of Warren Buffett, Bill Gross, and other long-term compounders.

Practical users almost never bet full Kelly. Betting more than the Kelly fraction increases volatility without increasing growth and raises the risk of ruin; even at exactly Kelly, drawdowns can be brutal — you have roughly a one-third chance of halving your bankroll before doubling it. "Half Kelly" sacrifices about a quarter of the growth rate for dramatically lower variance, and it accounts for model error: the formula assumes probabilities are known with certainty, which is never true in investing. Thorp called this discount for uncertainty essential.

The deeper transferable idea is that bet *size* is itself a decision, and the right size depends on both edge and odds. Most people treat each opportunity as bet-or-don't-bet; Kelly says the interesting question is how much, and that the optimal fraction grows with your edge and shrinks with the payoff odds. Applied broadly — to startups, to career bets, to time allocation — it argues for going big where edge is large and downside is survivable, and for the discipline of passing entirely when you cannot estimate an edge at all.
