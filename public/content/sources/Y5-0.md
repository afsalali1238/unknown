The Banach–Tarski paradox (Stefan Banach and Alfred Tarski, 1924) is a theorem of set-theoretic geometry: a solid ball in three-dimensional space can be decomposed into a finite number of pieces (as few as five) which can then be reassembled — using only rotations and translations, no stretching or adding points — into **two** identical copies of the original ball. In its strong form, any two bounded solid objects with interior points are interconvertible this way: "a pea can be chopped up and reassembled into the Sun."

It is a *veridical* paradox — provably true under standard mathematics, yet violently counterintuitive. The resolution lies in what the "pieces" are. They are not solid chunks with surfaces and volumes; they are infinitely scattered, non-measurable point sets whose volume cannot be defined at all. The ordinary intuition that rigid motions preserve volume applies only to measurable sets. Once the intermediate pieces have no well-defined volume, the volume bookkeeping never applies — nothing about volume conservation is contradicted, because there is no quantity attached to the pieces to conserve.

The proof depends essentially on the **axiom of choice** (via Hausdorff's paradoxical decomposition of the sphere and the group structure of rotations in 3D, which contains free subgroups). The axiom permits constructing non-measurable sets by making uncountably many choices with no definable rule. Notably, the paradox is impossible in dimensions one and two for finite decompositions — only the richer rotation groups of 3D+ space admit paradoxical decomposition (countably many pieces suffice even in lower dimensions). This dimensional boundary is itself a clue: the weirdness lives in the mathematics of infinite, structureless sets, not in geometry per se.

The theorem is philosophically significant as a stress test of axioms: Banach and Tarski themselves saw it as evidence about what set theory allows, and it motivated analysis of measurable sets and measure theory. As a mental model it teaches two durable lessons. First, the properties of whole objects (volume, mass, price, identity) need not attach to every arbitrary decomposition of them — categories that work at one granularity can fail at finer or infinite granularities. Second, strong axioms and powerful abstractions purchase results that are true but operationally meaningless: the decomposition pieces cannot be physically cut, described, or constructed, so the theorem is about the formal universe rather than material objects — a warning about which mathematical truths make claims on reality.
