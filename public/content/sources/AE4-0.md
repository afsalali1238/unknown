In *The Black Swan* (2007), Nassim Nicholas Taleb names the **narrative fallacy** as our compulsion to compress, simplify, and storify complex, random reality after the fact — weaving events into plausible causal stories that make them seem predictable, necessary, and intelligible, when they were largely contingent and invisible in advance. The fallacy is part of what Taleb calls the "Triplet of Opacity": an illusion of understanding (we think we comprehend a world far more random than our accounts admit), retrospective distortion (we assess history only after outcomes are known, rewriting uncertainty as inevitability), and overvaluation of facts and "experts" whose confident stories fit the past but fail out of sample.

The mechanisms are familiar cognitive ones. We are pattern-hungry: memory itself edits and organizes events into coherent plots, dropping contradictions and lucky accidents. We assign causes to luck — successful entrepreneurs are credited with brilliance, failed ones with stupidity, even when their decisions were indistinguishable before the draw. We build "histories" of markets, wars, and technologies as if outcomes flowed from a few named causes, ignoring the thousands of parallel paths that almost happened. The narrative reduces dimensionality: a story with a few actors, motives, and turning points is memorable and satisfying, while reality is high-dimensional and dominated by rare, consequential events (Black Swans) that no story constructed from ordinary precedent anticipated.

Taleb accepts the contradiction that he is telling stories to warn about stories: "You need a story to displace a story. Metaphors and stories are far more potent (alas) than ideas; they are also easier to remember." The point is not to stop narrating — humans are narrative animals and cannot — but to notice the *compression* and its costs. Narratives make us overconfident (history looks inevitable in hindsight, so we believe we can forecast forward), blind to silent evidence (we read the stories of winners and never meet the identical strategies of the eliminated), and vulnerable to Black Swans (models built on coherent past stories break when the rare event arrives).

The practical disciplines Taleb recommends: distinguish sensing from theorizing; seek raw data and disconfirming evidence rather than confirming plots; be wary of experts whose expertise is *storytelling* about closed systems (journalists, historians of markets, strategists) versus those with hard accountability and skin in the game; run your affairs to be robust to being wrong about the story — barbell strategies, convexity, avoiding ruin — since the story will be wrong exactly when it matters most; and keep an "antilibrary" of what you don't know, treating awareness of narrative compression itself as the defense. For builders, forecasters, and readers of any postmortem: the question to ask after every satisfying explanation is, *what did this look like before the fact — and how many near-identical setups ended differently?*
