Critical thinking is the deliberate process of evaluating information — your own beliefs included — to reach judgments that are better reasoned and less at the mercy of impulse, rhetoric, and bias. Samantha Agoos's TED-Ed lesson distills a five-step process usable on any decision or claim.

1. **Formulate your question.** Know precisely what you're trying to determine. Vague goals ("is this a good idea?") accept vague answers; specific questions ("will this option reduce costs within a year without raising risk?") direct attention to the evidence that matters. In decisions this means separating what you're really optimizing for from what merely feels salient.

2. **Gather your information.** Collect information from multiple sources, aware of their incentives. Knowing where claims come from — who benefits, what's omitted — lets you weigh rather than absorb. This is the stage to seek disconfirming input and base rates, not just supporting detail.

3. **Apply the information.** Ask what conclusions the evidence logically supports, and watch the structure of reasoning: what assumptions are being made? What follows *if* the claims are true? Distinguish concepts, interpretations, and goals; check whether the reasoning works by analogy to similar cases.

4. **Consider the implications.** A choice or belief has consequences beyond the immediate one. Who is helped, who is harmed, what are second- and third-order effects, what does this imply for future decisions? Even a logically sound conclusion with terrible implications deserves a second look; preferred options with hidden costs (short-term win, long-term lock-in) get flagged here.

5. **Explore other points of view.** Deliberately engage perspectives that contradict yours — why do smart people disagree? What would have to be true for them to be right? This step attacks confirmation bias directly and often reveals context your initial frame excluded.

The accompanying meta-lessons: none of the steps is cognitively natural — the brain prefers speed, consistency, and identity-protective beliefs — so critical thinking is a discipline practiced on paper, in conversation, and through checklists rather than an innate talent. Also, the cycle repeats: the process is iterative, with each step exposing new questions. Related habits include separating inference from observation, naming your assumptions, writing down reasoning so it can be inspected, and treating your own first conclusion as a hypothesis to stress-test rather than a verdict to defend.
