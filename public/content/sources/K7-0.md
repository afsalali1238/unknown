> Originally published in Nature Physics as "The ergodicity problem in economics" — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41567-019-0732-0 (textbook: Peters & Adamou, An Introduction to Ergodicity Economics, LML Press 2025).
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the author.

Ole Peters' ergodicity economics challenges one of the foundational assumptions of expected-utility theory: that the rational way to evaluate a risky prospect is to average outcomes across a *ensemble* of many parallel people (expected value). Peters argues this is only valid for **ergodic** processes — where the time average of one entity over a long period equals the ensemble average of many entities at one time. When a process is non-ergodic, the ensemble average tells you almost nothing about what happens to *you over time*.

The clean demonstration is a multiplicative gamble: a coin flip that increases your wealth by 50% on heads and decreases it by 40% on tails. The expected return per round is +5% ((50% − 40%)/2) — mathematically attractive; across a large ensemble, average wealth grows at 5% per round. But for *one person playing repeatedly*, the geometric return is 1.5 × 0.6 = 0.9 per pair of rounds: wealth multiplies by √0.9 ≈ 0.949 per round — it shrinks ~5% per round. Almost everyone in a large ensemble goes broke, even though ensemble-average wealth rises (driven by extremely rare, astronomically rich trajectories). Time average and ensemble average diverge. Multiplicative dynamics — which describe investing, leverage, compounding, and anything where outcomes scale with your current stake — are non-ergodic: absorbing barriers (ruin, zero) are reached eventually by almost all paths.

The consequences are substantial. Risk aversion stops being a psychological "utility" wrinkle and becomes rational mathematics: avoiding ruin is correct because dead/broke paths stop compounding forever. This reframes insurance, the equity premium, cooperation, and inequality: much of what economics modeled as irrational preferences or market puzzles follows directly from optimizing time-average growth under non-ergodic dynamics. Kelly betting (betting your edge proportional to edge/odds) emerges as the time-average-optimal strategy, connecting directly to growth-maximizing behavior without utility functions.

Practical takeaways for decision-making: (1) **Avoid absorbing barriers at all costs** — if a strategy can go to zero, ensemble averages lie about your experience; variance that includes ruin is categorically different from bounded downside. (2) Judge bets by what happens along *your* repeated path, not by average outcome across hypothetical others. (3) Position size and leverage matter as much as edge — a positive-expectation bet taken at too large a fraction guarantees eventual ruin under multiplicative dynamics. (4) Systems designed around aggregate statistics (portfolios vs. individual careers, population health vs. one body, mean outcomes vs. median paths) can recommend acts that are disastrous for almost every individual following them over time. The framework remains debated among economists, but as a mental model for serial risk-takers it is simple and durable: never make a bet where the time-average kills you, no matter how good the ensemble average looks.
