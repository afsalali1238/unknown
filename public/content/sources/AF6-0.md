Reed's law, articulated by David P. Reed in his 2001 Harvard Business Review article "The Law of the Pack," states that the utility of large networks — particularly social, group-forming networks (GFNs) — can scale **exponentially** with the number of participants, as 2^n.

The derivation is combinatorial. Sarnoff's law values a broadcast network linearly with its audience (n). Metcalfe's law values a communications network by its possible pairwise connections, ~n²/2. But once participants can form groups freely — chat rooms, communities, clans, projects, subreddits, group chats — the number of *possible groups* among n people is the number of non-trivial subsets: 2^n − n − 1. Each subset is a potential community with shared purpose, conversation, or coordination value. Even if the utility of each possible group is small, the group-forming term eventually dominates the economics of the system.

Reed's own words: "Even Metcalfe's law understates the value created by a group-forming network as it grows. If you add up all the potential two-person groups, three-person groups, and so on that those members could form, the number of possible groups equals 2^n. So the value of a GFN increases exponentially, in proportion to 2^n."

The business implication is architectural: platforms become valuable not just by connecting people but by making group formation frictionless. A feed is Metcalfe; easy-to-create communities are Reed. Platforms that let users cluster around shared interests, tools, or identities (early AOL chat rooms, Usenet, Slack workspaces, Discord servers, Facebook groups) tap the exponential; platforms that only connect pairs leave most of the value on the table. Network effects at the group level are also harder to copy — communities carry accumulated content, norms, and relationships.

The law has critics. Andrew Odlyzko and others argue that both Metcalfe and Reed overstate value because humans have cognitive limits: Dunbar's number bounds the meaningful relationships one person can maintain, so almost all of the 2^n possible groups are worthless to any given user, and aggregate value grows much more slowly (Odlyzko and Briscoe suggested ~n·log(n)). The synthesis for platform strategy: group-forming tools unlock a qualitatively different and larger layer of value than pair communication, but only the *activated, well-moderated, sticky* groups count — the combinatorial potential must be converted into actual communities, which is a design and governance problem as much as a growth one.
