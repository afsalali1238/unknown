*Leviathan, or The Matter, Forme and Power of a Commonwealth Ecclesiasticall and Civil* is a work of social and political theory by the English philosopher Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679), published in 1651, with a revised Latin edition in 1668. Its name derives from the chaotic sea serpent of the Hebrew Bible. Written during the English Civil War, it is one of the earliest and most influential examples of social contract theory.

Hobbes begins by imagining human life without government — the "state of nature." With no common power to keep order, he argues, individuals are roughly equal in their ability to harm one another, and competition, distrust, and glory produce perpetual conflict: "during the time men live without a common power to keep them all in awe, they are in that condition which is called war; and such a war as is of every man against every man." In that condition, in his most famous sentence, "the life of man" is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." There is no industry, no agriculture, no navigation, no art, no letters, no society — because none can be secure in the fruit of their labor.

The escape is the social contract. Reason suggests "articles of peace" — laws of nature commanding people to seek peace where possible, and to lay down their unlimited right to everything *if others do likewise*. People therefore covenant with one another to authorize a sovereign — the "Leviathan," an "artificial man" or "mortal God" — that holds the collective power to enforce the agreement. Hobbes's sovereign is absolute: because the contract is among the subjects, not between subjects and ruler, the sovereign cannot breach it; law is what the sovereign commands; and division of power is fatal, since competing authorities recreate the war of all against all. Hobbes prefers monarchy for its unity of interest but allows that an assembly may hold the sovereignty — what matters is that it be undivided and strong enough to guarantee security.

The book's famous frontispiece, etched by Abraham Bosse with Hobbes's input, depicts a giant crowned figure rising over a landscape, its body composed of hundreds of tiny individuals facing inward — the many compacted into one — clutching a sword (civil power) and a crosier (ecclesiastical power). The quotation beneath is from the Book of Job: "There is no power on earth to be compared to him."

Leviathan scandalized its age: by grounding political obligation in self-interest rather than divine right, and by subordinating church to state, Hobbes was accused of atheism and absolutism alike. Yet the book founded modern political philosophy in the form Locke, Rousseau, and Kant would inherit — the attempt to derive legitimate government from a contract among the governed rather than from the order of the cosmos.
