Mr. Market is Benjamin Graham's parable from *The Intelligent Investor* (1949), the metaphor Warren Buffett has called the best book ever written on investing. Imagine you own a private business with a partner, Mr. Market, who every day offers to buy your share or sell you his at a price. The wonderful fact: Mr. Market's services come with no obligation to accept. The dangerous fact: he is emotionally unstable.

Some days he is euphoric, seeing only favorable developments, and quotes a wildly high price; other days he is depressed, sees trouble everywhere, and quotes a wildly low price. He is there to serve you, not to guide you — you may *use* his prices, but you should never *be governed* by his moods. If he quotes you far below value, you have the option to buy; far above value, you can sell; in between, ignore him. He doesn't care; he will be back tomorrow with a new quote.

The lesson is market psychology and discipline in one frame: the prices of securities oscillate around underlying business value like a mood-driven partner, driven in the short run by sentiment, fear, greed, and liquidity, and in the long run by business performance. The investor's edge is comparing price to value (the job of analysis) rather than predicting Mr. Market's mood (impossible). Sell when he offers greed-level prices; buy from panic; never let his emotional state become yours. His manias become contagious through the surrounding crowd — everyone bullish at the top, bearish at the bottom — precisely the moments where discipline pays.

Buffett operationalized the parable: "be fearful when others are greedy and greedy when others are fearful"; market fluctuations are an opportunity, not a referendum on you. Modern indexing and automated systems still run on the Graham premise for active investors: the market is there to be exploited for those who know what the businesses are worth, and ignored otherwise. The emotional structure also generalizes beyond markets — any environment with prices set by mood (real estate, talent markets, attention economies) rewards those with an independent value estimate, and punishes those who let the quote set the belief.
