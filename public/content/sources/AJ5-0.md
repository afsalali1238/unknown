Signaling theory, introduced by Michael Spence in his 1973 paper "Job Market Signaling" (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*), describes how parties with private information communicate it in markets where the information cannot be directly verified before a transaction. Spence shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences (with George Akerlof and Joseph Stiglitz) for the analysis of markets with asymmetric information.

The setup is a hiring market. An employer cannot observe a job candidate's future productivity before hiring — only the candidate knows whether they are, in Spence's model, high-ability or low-ability. The employer would pay more for high-ability workers but cannot tell them apart, so everyone is paid a pooled average wage and the high-ability workers are underpaid. Spence's question: can a high-ability worker send a *signal* that separates them? Education is the answer — but only under a specific condition.

For a signal to be credible, it must be **cheaper for the high-type than the low-type**. If finishing a degree costs the same in effort and time for a low-productivity person as for a high-productivity one, low types would just buy the degree too, and employers would learn nothing from it. But if capable people find the coursework easier, tolerate the material better, and finish with less pain, then education functions as a filter: high types complete more of it, low types drop out, and employers can rationally pay a wage premium for credentials even if the education itself does nothing to increase productivity. Spence's point is not that education is useless — it is that credentials carry value partly *as information*, separating types who cannot otherwise be told apart.

This is the deep distinction between a signal and ordinary talk ("cheap talk"): talk is free, so anyone can claim to be high-ability; a credible signal costs real resources, and costs differently across types. The logic generalizes enormously: warranties signal product quality (offering a generous warranty is cheap for a reliable manufacturer, ruinous for a maker of lemons); advertising signals confidence in the product even if it conveys no facts; dividends signal management's belief in future cash; burning money in a startup launch signals commitment; conspicuous consumption signals wealth; engagement rings and other costly courtship gestures signal long-term intent.

In each case the signal is wasteful from the point of view of the world that already knows the truth — Spence himself called it a costly adjustment to ignorance. But where information asymmetry exists, the parties with good attributes are forced to pay to prove them, and the structure of the cost — not the content of the message — is what makes the proof convincing. For observers, the useful question about any impressive display is not "what does it say?" but "what would it cost someone who didn't have the quality to fake it?" If the answer is "about the same," the display carries no information at all.
