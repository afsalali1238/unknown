> Farnam Street's *The Great Mental Models* project — https://fs.blog/tgmm/
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the author.

The Great Mental Models (Shane Parrish, Rhiannon Beaubien; a two-volume book project and the Farnam Street model library) organizes the core "thinking tools" that work across domains, on the Munger principle that big problems are interdisciplinary while education is departmentalized.

Volume 1 covers general thinking concepts:

- **The map is not the territory** — representations always simplify and omit; models are tools, not reality; keep checking maps against the world.
- **Circle of competence** — decisions improve the closer they sit to what you truly understand; ego and outside pressure push you outside.
- **First principles thinking** — reason from irreducible truths rather than analogy.
- **Thought experiment** — explore possibilities by running structured "what if" reasoning (Einstein's light-beam, Schrödinger's setup).
- **Second-order thinking** — "and then what?": consequences of consequences.
- **Probabilistic thinking** — Bayesian updating, base rates; living in probabilities rather than certainties.
- **Inversion** — solve backward from failure.
- **Occam's razor** (favor simple explanations) and **Hanlon's razor** (incompetence before malice).

Volume 2 covers systems and limits: feedback loops, equilibrium, bottlenecks, scale, reduction vs. emergence, constraints, and **luck** (distinguish signal from randomness), with supporting ideas like causality, correlations as traps, and irreducibility.

The method the project teaches: learn models deliberately (one at a time, with examples); apply them by asking "what models apply here?" when facing a decision; check each against others — models compound when several converge on one answer; and review where a model let you see what others missed or where it misled. Models are not a checklist for knowing answers; they are lenses that keep the thinker from the blind spots that single-domain training and cognitive bias create.
