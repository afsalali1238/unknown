The Overton window is the range of ideas and arguments politically acceptable to the mainstream population at a given time — also called the "window of discourse." It is named after Joseph Overton, an American policy analyst and senior vice president at the Mackinac Center for Public Policy, who proposed that the political viability of an idea depends mainly on whether it falls within this acceptability range, not on the individual preferences of politicians.

Overton described a spectrum from "more free" to "less free" with respect to government intervention, arranged vertically to avoid confusion with left and right. As the spectrum moves or expands, an idea at a given location may become more or less politically acceptable. Politicians typically act freely only inside the window: they can recommend policies within it without seeming extreme and risking office. After Overton's death, his colleague Joseph Lehman developed the idea and named it after him.

Lehman stresses a common misconception: "Lawmakers are actually in the business of detecting where the window is, and then moving to be in accordance with it." Politicians are followers of acceptability, not authors of it; the window is moved by thinkers, activists, movements, media, and the slow drift of public opinion. He presents the concept as a neutral description — "the same way that gravity explains why something falls to the earth. I can use gravity to drop an anvil on your head, but that would be wrong. I could also use gravity to throw you a life preserver."

Joshua Treviño postulated six degrees of acceptance through which an idea moves: **unthinkable → radical → acceptable → sensible → popular → policy**. The window itself comprises the politically possible — the ideas from "acceptable" downward. Proponents of policies outside the window seek to expand it by publicizing proposals similar to or even more extreme than their own (pulling the window toward them), or to shift it by making opposed ideas seem unacceptable; defenders of the status quo seek to narrow it by framing alternatives as beyond the pale.

The concept explains a recurring persuasion strategy: to make a position feel normal, you need not argue for it directly — you can introduce a position more extreme still, which re-anchors the center. Ideas that a decade ago were "unthinkable" can become "sensible" without any single vote changing a law, because public tolerance moved first. It has since been applied beyond politics — to corporate culture, scientific consensus, and institutional norms — wherever public opinion gates what may be proposed without reputational cost. It is closely related to anchoring, creeping normality, and the normalization process in sociology.
