Eternalism, or the block-universe view, holds that all times — past, present, and future — are equally real, and that time is a dimension analogous to space: the universe is a four-dimensional "block" of spacetime, with events laid out along the time axis as cities are laid out along a highway. Nothing in physics singles out a moving "now"; different moments exist the way different places exist, and the flow of time is a feature of experience rather than of the world. It opposes **presentism** — only the present exists — and the growing-block view — past and present exist, future does not yet.

The strongest argument for eternalism comes from special relativity's **relativity of simultaneity**. Observers moving relative to one another disagree about which distant events happen "at the same time," and there is no physical basis for declaring one observer's present the true one. Two events can be simultaneous in one frame, ordered one way in another, and ordered the reverse in a third — as long as they are outside each other's light cones (spacelike separated). Hilary Putnam (1967) argued that if reality is frame-independent and "what is real for me here-now" includes events simultaneous with me, then Rietdijk–Putnam-style reasoning forces future events to be already real; many philosophers of physics conclude presentism is incompatible with relativity, short of positing an unobservable privileged reference frame.

What relativity *does* preserve is causal structure: within your light cone, past and future are agreed by every observer. Time ordering of cause and effect never flips; only the distant "elsewhere" lacks a frame-independent present. So the block does not erase causality — it geometrizes it.

Eternalism clashes powerfully with the manifest image: we experience a moving present, an open future, and a fixed past. The block view replies that the "now" is like "here" — an indexical marking a position in the block, not a property of the cosmos; the openness of the future is epistemic (we don't know future events) rather than ontological (they are indeterminate). Einstein, consoling the family of Michele Besso, wrote that the distinction between past, present, and future is "only a stubbornly persistent illusion."

The view raises sharp questions for free will and agency (does the future "already exist"?), for the arrow of time (why do we experience flow one way if the block is static? — answered partly by entropy and memory formation), and for quantum mechanics (collapse interpretations seem to require a preferred foliation). Whether eternalism is the final word remains debated, with presentists and others defending alternatives compatible with relativity. Its enduring power is the shift from "time as a river that flows" to "time as a geometry in which events stand" — a perspective that reshapes how one thinks about change, persistence, and what "now" could possibly mean across a universe with no master clock.
