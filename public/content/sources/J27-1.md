Bayes' theorem is the rule for updating beliefs with evidence: posterior belief = prior belief × likelihood ratio of the new evidence. Its content is that good thinking moves probabilities, it doesn't establish certainties — and that where you start (the **prior**) heavily constrains where evidence takes you. Farnam Street's "deadweight" framing emphasizes that people carry prior beliefs like deadweight — outdated, inherited, or emotionally anchored probabilities that they fail to update when the world changes.

The common failures: (1) **no explicit priors** — people react to each story as if from nothing, missing base rates (a "successful" startup headline against a failure rate of 90% of venture-backed companies). (2) **Priors that never move** — beliefs treated as identity rather than probability; each new piece of evidence is rationalized away rather than multiplying the odds. (3) **Wrong likelihood weighting** — treating vivid, memorable, or recent evidence as much stronger than it actually is (availability, salience), while ignoring boring statistical evidence. (4) **Beliefs inherited rather than calibrated** — priors come from parents, tribe, culture, or era; unless deliberately tested, they remain the framework through which evidence is interpreted.

Discipline: estimate the base rate before looking at the specific case ("how common is this generally?"); update incrementally and quantitatively, asking "how much more likely is this evidence if I'm right than if I'm wrong?"; treat strongly held views as bets to be revisited rather than property; and expose yourself to evidence before your identity forms around a position. Good forecasters (Tetlock's superforecasters) are distinguished less by intelligence than by Bayesian habits: frequent small updates, willingness to say "about 65%", and tracking their calibration.

The meta-lesson: the weight of evidence needed to change a mind should be proportional to the strength of the prior — but priors that were calibrated in one environment (old regime, old industry, old self) are deadweight in a changed one, and clinging to them is the failure mode behind most failure-to-pivot, whether in markets, companies, or personal life.
