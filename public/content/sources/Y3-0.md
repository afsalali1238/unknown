Consciousness is the most familiar and most puzzling feature of mind: the fact that there is *something it is like* to perceive, feel, or think — the redness of red, the ache of pain, the flavor of experience. David Chalmers (1995) distinguished the **easy problems** of consciousness from the **hard problem**. The easy problems (not actually easy) concern objective functions: how the brain integrates information, directs attention, reports mental states, controls behavior, distinguishes wake from sleep. All of these are things a mechanism could in principle do; progress in neuroscience and AI consists largely of explaining them. The hard problem is: why and how does any of that physical processing *feel like something from the inside*? Why isn't all of it done "in the dark," without subjective experience?

The difficulty is the apparent **explanatory gap** (Joseph Levine): physical/functional descriptions explain structure and dynamics — what neurons do — but no description of structure and dynamics seems to entail the presence of experience. We can know every physical fact about a bat's echolocation and arguably still not know what echolocating feels like to the bat (Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?", 1974); Jackson's imagined color scientist Mary knows all the physics of color before first seeing red, yet seems to learn something new. These thought experiments argue that subjective experience cannot be read off third-person data.

Positions spread across a spectrum. **Physicalists** hold consciousness is entirely physical, and that the gap is epistemic — we lack the right concepts or explanations, not new ontology (Dennett: qualia as conceived do not exist; illusionism: experience as naively described is a mischaracterization of brain-generated representations). **Dualists** (property dualism following Chalmers) argue experience may be fundamental, or necessitated by principles beyond current physics, such as psychophysical laws linking information to experience. **Panpsychist** views take experience as a universal feature of matter, complex human consciousness arising from combination. Biological theories (Searle), integrated information theory (Tononi's Φ), global workspace (Dehaene), predictive processing, and higher-order theories compete to mark the boundary of consciousness differently.

The SEP survey notes features any account must address: qualitative character, subjectivity, unity of experience, transparency (we experience through experience, not it as an object), and dynamic flow. The functional question — why consciousness exists at all, what it contributes to flexible control, social coordination, and integrated decision-making — remains contested, with epiphenomenalists arguing experience may have no causal role of its own. Practically, the hard problem frames every debate about machine consciousness: passing functional tests (behavior, reports, even neural correlates) does not obviously settle whether anything is felt — the boundary between solving the easy problems and the hard one is the central open question of mind.
