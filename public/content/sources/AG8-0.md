Raymond Loewy (1893–1986) was a French-born American industrial designer whose seven-decade career earned him the press nicknames "The Man Who Shaped America," "The Father of Streamlining," and "The Father of Industrial Design." He designed or restyled the Shell, Exxon, TWA, and former BP logos; the Greyhound Scenicruiser bus; the Coca-Cola bottle redesign and vending machines; the Lucky Strike cigarette package; Coldspot refrigerators; the Studebaker Avanti and Champion; the Pennsylvania Railroad's streamlined locomotives; the livery of Air Force One; and even the interiors of NASA's Apollo and Skylab spacecraft. *Time* featured him on its cover in October 1949.

Loewy's signature contribution to design theory was a principle he called **MAYA: Most Advanced Yet Acceptable**. The idea is that the most successful new design is the one that pushes novelty as far as possible *without* exceeding what the public is psychologically ready to accept. Pure familiarity bores; pure novelty frightens or confuses. The commercial optimum lies at the boundary between the two: as advanced (different, innovative, striking) as the audience's tolerance for the unfamiliar will allow.

Loewy arrived at the principle through his streamlining work. He noticed that the public embraced dramatic new shapes only when they could be connected to familiar visual ideas; designs that were technically superior but too strange failed in the marketplace, while designs that were merely conventional lost to competitors that felt fresher. He advised selling "the new" through recognizable silhouettes — which is why his radical-looking locomotives and appliances still read as *machines people already understood*.

For product designers, MAYA functions as a adoption-rate constraint: innovation is not maximized by the best engineering but by the most *tolerable* change. Once a design language becomes familiar, the acceptable frontier moves — yesterday's "most advanced yet acceptable" becomes today's baseline, and the next product can push further. (Loewy is also associated with a related principle of design fatigue, expressed in his "ugly wave" observation that product aesthetics need periodic disruption before buyers tire of a shape.)

The principle anticipates later research on the mere-exposure effect, neophobia in taste, and the diffusion of innovations: Everett Rogers' finding that an innovation's rate of adoption depends not only on its relative advantage but on its compatibility with existing values and habits. MAYA remains a standard reference in industrial design, interaction design, and product marketing.
