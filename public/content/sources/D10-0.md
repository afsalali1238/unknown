> House-curated overview of Zoltan Pozsar's "Bretton Woods III" thesis (Credit Suisse, March 2022).
> Archived 2026-09-06 for personal offline reading. All rights remain with the cited author.

In March 2022, Zoltan Pozsar — Credit Suisse's closely followed money-markets strategist — published "Bretton Woods III," arguing that the freezing of Russia's foreign exchange reserves after the invasion of Ukraine was an epochal shock to the global monetary system, not merely a sanctions event.

His framework names three orders:

- **Bretton Woods I (1944–1971):** gold-backed, with the dollar convertible to gold at $35/oz; collapsed when U.S. spending (Vietnam, Great Society) made the gold window unsustainable and Nixon closed it.
- **Bretton Woods II (1971–2022):** the "inside money" system — paper dollar reserves, U.S. Treasuries as the global safe asset, with no commodity anchor. Central banks held claims on the U.S. state, and global trade ran through dollar payment rails.
- **Bretton Woods III (his predicted future):** a world in which **commodity collateral** — "outside money" anchored to real stuff, gold, and commodities — rises again as central banks and sovereigns diversify away from claims that can be frozen. Money is never purely "inside" (claims on states) after reserves have been weaponized; it must be nobody's liability.

Pozsar's mechanics: the G7 freezing of Russia's reserves demonstrated, more than any prior sanctions episode, that sovereign FX reserves in dollars/euros/yen are assets only at the issuer's forbearance. Countries managing geopolitical risk now have incentive to hold reserves that can't be expropriated — gold, and increasingly claims on critical commodities. Meanwhile the inflation regime shifted: commodity shocks, supply chain rewiring (friend-shoring), and commodity inventories as strategic assets all reverse the cheap-goods era that kept rates low. He forecast a commodity- and collateral-heavy monetary future, with the renminbi emerging as a trading currency anchored to Chinese commodity demand, and the dollar sharing rather than owning reserve status.

Critics made several points: much of Pozsar's note described short-term money-market plumbing (spreads on margin calls and commodity-basis trades) extrapolated into long history; actual reserve allocation data moved slowly — gold buying rose sharply but dollar share of global reserves fell only modestly, and renminbi's share stayed small; commodity currencies can't replace Treasuries because commodities are hard to settle in, expensive to store, and price-volatile; and monetary dominance rests on deep legal and institutional infrastructure, not just payment choice.

The durable content regardless of the timeline: the weaponization of dollar infrastructure changed the conversation about reserve assets permanently; central-bank gold accumulation since 2022 reached all-time highs; "outside money" (gold, real assets) re-entered institutional portfolios as insurance rather than legacy; and the episode is now the standard reference for the connection between geopolitics, collateral, and money. Pozsar's skill set as a strategist was identifying where plumbing events reveal regime change — and in this case the freeze-and-response did shift official behavior even if BWIII unfolds over decades rather than weeks.
